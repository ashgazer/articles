#### Introduction

## What’s in it for me? Navigate life’s hardest disruptions with clearer thinking, steadier emotions, and practical tools.

A single moment can split your life into before and after. One phone call, one diagnosis, one breakup, one mistake – and suddenly, everything changes. The world around you seems to carry on as usual, yet you're left clinging on for dear life. So what do you do when your world is turned upside down?

Do you try to grin and bear it? Tough it out? Just move on? If you've ever been through a life-shattering experience, you'll know that these kinds of solutions often ring hollow. Life-altering change demands more than simple platitudes. But when you're overwhelmed and struggling to stay afloat, finding a way forward can feel impossible.

The good news is that you're far from alone in these feelings. No matter the circumstances, people navigating major life changes tend to fall into similar traps – becoming numb, spiraling into rumination, clinging to old scripts, or shutting others out. Yet they also share a remarkable capacity to adapt. Across very different situations, many discover surprisingly similar paths forward: shifts in perspective that restore a sense of possibility, practices that soften shame, and small choices that gradually rebuild meaning.

In this Blink, you’ll follow a handful of vivid personal stories – people blindsided by life’s sharp turns – and watch how their stories reveal our capacity to adapt. Along the way, you’ll pick up practical ways to zoom out, soften shame with self-compassion, and reframe what your new reality means, so you can begin moving forward even before certainty returns. Let’s begin with Olivia, whose life changed overnight – and the kind of denial that kept her afloat just long enough to heal.

#### Chapter 1 of 6

## Denial can sometimes steady you until you can accept reality

In October of 2018, Olivia Lewis went to bed expecting an ordinary morning. Instead, she woke in the middle of the night to an intense jolt in her body, fell from her bed, and found she couldn’t move or call for help. Hours later, her roommate discovered her on the floor and called 911. When Olivia woke up in the hospital, she still couldn’t speak or move her body.

Refusing to give up, her aunt found a way to reach her. Using an alphabet board and a series of blinks, letter by letter, Olivia found a way to communicate again. Doctors explained that a brain-stem stroke had caused locked-in syndrome: Olivia’s mind was intact, but almost all voluntary movement was gone. When something this devastating happens, denial can sometimes act as a psychological life raft – and in Olivia's case, she clung to it with everything she had. She didn’t deny that she’d had a stroke. What she resisted was what it might mean for her future.

She became fixated on the story of Kate Allatt, another locked-in stroke survivor who had recovered quickly. Olivia treated Kate’s recovery as a blueprint for her own, and that belief gave her the hope she needed to push through grueling rehabilitation. But denial can become a problem when it ties us to a single outcome. Olivia avoided stories that challenged her expectations and grew frustrated when her progress didn't match the timeline she'd imagined. Beneath that frustration was a deeper fear. Before her stroke, after years of self-doubt, she had finally begun to feel accepted, confident, and good enough.

The stroke threatened not only her future, but also the version of herself she had worked so hard to become. A change of scenery helped. At an elite rehab center far from home, Olivia realized her therapists weren’t judging her the way she assumed others would. As she relaxed into being imperfect and vulnerable, she began building a sturdier identity – one grounded less in impressing people and more in what mattered in her new reality.

Denial helped her survive the first shock, but acceptance became possible once her self-worth wasn’t dependent on returning to “Old Olivia. ” When your identity is grounded in values rather than outcomes, acceptance becomes less frightening – and far more sustainable. #### Chapter 2 of 6

## Your future expands when you allow new possible selves

Locked in a windowless basement cell with almost nothing to do but wait, he watched the days blur together. Before long, his world felt as small as the room he was trapped inside. How did he end up there? Well, a month after his 16th birthday, he had gone to a mall with a friend and a few older guys.

Tired of feeling powerless in the world, he asked for a gun. In the parking lot, he approached a man sleeping in his car, told him to get out, and drove away with his wallet, keys, and car. He was arrested the next day, tried as an adult, and sentenced to nine years in prison. Behind bars, he lived with constant fear – not just of violence, but of the person he might become. Psychologists call these imagined futures "possible selves": the people we hope to become, expect to become, or fear becoming. After a life-shattering change, those futures can collapse into each other.

And when the feared version of yourself is the only one you can picture, it can start to feel inevitable. But Dwayne's feared sense of self began to change when he met a man named Bilal in the prison yard. Bilal followed a strict routine, and taught younger inmates how to box so they could protect themselves. In a place that rewarded toughness and self-preservation, he stood for something different: discipline, dignity, and care. For Dwayne, seeing that kind of character in such a harsh environment opened up a new vision of what life in prison could look like. That vision expanded later during a stint in solitary confinement.

There, Dwayne discovered an underground network that passed books from cell to cell. One day, a poetry anthology called The Black Poets made its way into his hands. Through it, he discovered Etheridge Knight – a writer who had served time in prison and transformed his experience into art. For Dwayne, Knight became proof that someone with a past like his could still build a meaningful identity and future. Inspired by that possibility, Dwayne immersed himself in reading and writing. He began writing every day, eventually producing more than a thousand poems in a single year at a supermax prison.

Sometimes a future expands the moment you discover a new possible self – and take the first step toward becoming it. Early in 2020, jjournalist Matt Gutman was in his kitchen making breakfast for his kids when his phone rang. A helicopter had gone down in Southern California.

#### Chapter 3 of 6

## Distance turns spirals into manageable thoughts

Kobe Bryant was reportedly on board, and Matt needed to get on the air immediately. As he drove toward the scene, his body shifted into high alert. His heart pounded. His hands trembled.

His chest tightened, and every breath felt strained. By then, Matt had spent two decades as a journalist. He'd also spent much of that time dealing with panic attacks. The trigger was often the same: the fear of failing in public, especially when the stakes were high and every word felt like it might be judged. That day, under the pressure of live reporting, Matt shared a detail that later turned out to be wrong. His network suspended him for a month.

Afterward, his mind did what many minds do after a mistake: it got stuck. He replayed the moment again and again, searching for what he should have done differently. He refreshed news stories, looked for reactions online, and treated every negative comment as confirmation of his worst fears. The more he tried to think his way out of the situation, the deeper he sank into it. That loop is called rumination. It feels productive – as if you're solving a problem – but it often does the opposite.

It amplifies fear and shame, narrows your perspective, and convinces you that one more round of analysis will finally bring relief. What ultimately helped Matt wasn't trying to suppress the thoughts. It was learning to step back from them. He began to see panic as an overprotective alarm system rather than a personal flaw. That shift softened his self-judgment and made it easier to focus on what would actually help. The same approach can work for any emotional spiral.

Start by naming what you're feeling. Treat yourself with the same kindness you'd offer a friend. Then create some distance by widening your perspective: take a walk, spend time in nature, distract yourself with another activity, or talk to someone you trust. Often, a little distance is enough to loosen rumination's grip and help you move forward again.

#### Chapter 4 of 6

## Attachment can harden after loss, and soften again with care

Tara Sharp adored her father, Felix. He was a generous artist who gave endlessly to his community. But at home, his moods could shift without warning, and Tara learned to stay constantly alert to the signals he was sending. She got good grades, stayed out of trouble, and learned to keep her own needs small.

Love felt safest when it was carefully managed. Then, during Tara's first week of college, Felix died by suicide. The loss changed the way she approached relationships. She threw herself into school and work, pulled away from friends, and ended a serious relationship. Distance started to feel like protection. Years later, Tara met Ben.

He carried his own emotional walls, and in some ways that made them a good match. Neither expected too much vulnerability from the other. Together, they built a life in Sonoma County – a marriage, a small winery, and a comfortable routine – while keeping some of the deeper parts of themselves tucked away. Then their first daughter, Cece, was born. For Tara, the experience was a revelation. The bond she felt with her daughter was intense and immediate.

And that mattered, because it showed her that despite everything she'd been through, she was still capable of attaching deeply to someone. But that sense of security didn't last. During her second pregnancy, the pressure started to mount. Tara was severely sick. The winery demanded constant attention. Ben coped by throwing himself into work.

Then a late ultrasound revealed that their unborn daughter had a serious heart defect connected to a hidden family history. Tara and Ben chose a high-risk surgical plan. When their daughter Ellie was born, she was rushed straight to intensive care. Even after coming home, she needed tube feeding and constant monitoring. Tara found herself shutting down. She felt numb, disconnected, and deeply depressed.

For a long time, Tara moved through each day in survival mode. Then, after one hospital visit, something shifted. Driving home, she glanced in the rearview mirror and saw Ellie looking healthier than she had in months. For the first time in a long while, she allowed herself to imagine that things might be okay. When she pulled into the garage, she broke down. From there, she rebuilt attachment in small, deliberate steps.

When holding Ellie in the rocking chair, she began looking into her eyes for a few seconds at a time. When fear told her to pull away, she practiced staying. She let herself enjoy ordinary moments again – buying tiny outfits, lingering at bedtime, saying "I love you" without immediately bracing for loss. Little by little, those choices added up.

What once felt dangerous began to feel bearable. Then, eventually, trustworthy. Grief had taught Tara to keep love at arm's length. Healing came when she discovered that the safest way forward wasn't through distance, but through learning to stay close.

#### Chapter 5 of 6

## A major change can rewrite the beliefs you grew up with

Ingrid Rojas Contreras grew up in Bogotá surrounded by stories of spiritual healing. There were tales of her grandfather's rituals, her mother's gifts, and a family lineage that seemed touched by something supernatural. But alongside those stories was a rule her mother made very clear: don't share them outside the family. The reason wasn't that the stories were untrue.

It was that the outside world could respond with ridicule, hostility, or even violence. Keeping these stories private was a way of staying safe. However, the longer she kept the stories secret, the more she started to feel a sense of shame about them. Why conceal them unless there was inherently something wrong with them? Years later, while living in Chicago, Ingrid crashed her bike into an open car door and hit the pavement hard. When she got back up, something was wrong.

She couldn't recognize the streets around her. She couldn't remember basic facts about her own life. She could still function, but parts of her past had simply vanished. And yet, something unexpected happened. Alongside the memory loss came a strange sense of relief. Without her usual internal narrative running in the background, she felt lighter.

It was as if she'd been carrying a burden for years without realizing it. As she recovered, her memories began returning in fragments. Eventually, her family stories came back too. Excited and energized, she started sharing them with friends. She spoke openly about them – proudly, even – as if the old family rule had never existed. Then one day, the rule resurfaced.

“I'm not supposed to talk about this... ” And with it came something else: shame. But this time, the feeling was different. Ingrid could remember the shame, but she couldn't fully feel it.

It was like looking at an old scar. She could see where it had been, but it no longer hurt in quite the same way. That distance gave her a new perspective. Maybe the rule had never meant there was something wrong with her family or their traditions. Maybe it was simply a practical response to a world that could be cruel toward things it didn't understand. Somewhere along the way, she'd confused caution with condemnation.

That's one of the hidden opportunities inside major change. Sometimes it interrupts old beliefs long enough for you to examine them. And sometimes, the most liberating realization is that a rule you've carried for years doesn't mean what you thought it did. Once you see that, you're free to rewrite the story you've been living inside. Maryann Gray felt like her life was opening up. At twenty-two, she’d dropped out of grad school, defied her parents’ plans, and moved toward a freer, more authentic version of herself.

Then, driving back to her apartment one day, a child ran into the road. She hit him. His name was Brian, and he died. At twenty-two, Maryann Gray felt like her life was finally opening up.

#### Chapter 6 of 6

## Self-compassion and community can loosen the grip of shame

She had dropped out of grad school, pushed back against her parents' expectations, and begun moving toward a version of herself that felt freer and more authentic. Then, one day, everything changed. Driving back to her apartment, a child suddenly ran into the road in front of her. She hit him.

His name was Brian, and he died. Everyone around her told her the same thing: it was a tragic accident. Maryann hadn't been speeding. She wasn't distracted or impaired. There was nothing she reasonably could have done to prevent it. But that explanation brought little comfort.

Her mind couldn't accept that something so devastating could happen for no reason. If she was a good person trying to do the right thing, how could this happen? Slowly, she began to turn the blame inward. The danger, she decided, wasn't the road. It was her. For years, she carried that belief.

She withdrew from the world, scrutinized her every action, and lived with an inner voice that insisted she was somehow fundamentally flawed. Part of what trapped her was a belief many of us share: the idea that the world is basically fair, that good actions lead to good outcomes, and bad outcomes must have a cause. Psychologists call this the "just-world hypothesis. " It can be comforting because it makes life feel predictable. But when tragedy strikes, that same belief can turn against us. If everything happens for a reason, then suffering can start to feel like evidence that we've done something wrong.

Decades later, Maryann watched public outrage unfold after an elderly driver accidentally crashed into a crowded farmers market, killing several people. As she followed the story, she recognized something familiar. People desperately wanted someone to blame. Because if the crash was truly accidental, then it could happen to anyone. The experience prompted Maryann to share her own story publicly. To her surprise, she wasn't met with condemnation.

She was met with compassion. That response opened a door she hadn't known was there. If she could feel empathy for another person who had caused accidental harm, perhaps she could offer some of that same grace to herself. Around that time, she learned about the ancient cities of refuge – special places described in the Hebrew Bible for people who had accidentally caused someone's death. Instead of facing revenge or being cast out from society, they could seek shelter there and remain part of a community while they rebuilt their lives. The idea resonated deeply with Maryann.

Since no such refuge existed today, she helped create one herself: a community for people carrying the same kind of unbearable guilt she had lived with for decades. Some events don't happen for a reason. They're simply painful. And sometimes, healing begins when we stop searching for meaning in the tragedy itself, and start finding it in how we respond – with compassion, connection, and a renewed willingness to engage with life.

#### Conclusion

## Final summary

The main takeaway of this Blink to The Other Side of Change by Maya Shankar is that even when change feels like it has shattered your life, it can also open up a new path forward. Not one built on control or certainty, but on perspective, connection, and choice. When our lives are turned upside down, it's natural to cling to denial, get trapped in rumination, pull away from the people we love, or hold on to beliefs and shame we've carried for years. These reactions aren't signs that something is wrong with you.

They're deeply human responses to overwhelm. But as the stories in this Blink show, they're not permanent. With a little distance, fear can loosen its grip. With small acts of courage and connection, trust can begin to grow again. And with self-compassion, community, and a willingness to imagine a different future, it's possible to stop treating pain as proof that you're broken and start building a life around what matters most. Change may not be the ending you wanted.

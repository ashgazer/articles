#### Introduction

## What’s in it for me? Learn how to turn adversity into success through eight principles.

Robert Reffkin is the founder and CEO of Compass, a real estate technology company worth billions of dollars. On paper, his story looks like a straight line of success. Raised by a single mother with little money, he started a DJ business in high school that earned more than $100,000. He used those earnings to help pay his way through Columbia University, graduating in just two and a half years.

He went on to build a career that included stints at McKinsey, Lazard, the White House, and Goldman Sachs. He founded a nonprofit for first-generation college students and even ran 50 marathons in all 50 US states to raise $1 million for charity. But that's only part of the story. Reffkin carried a C average through high school and college. He was rejected for hundreds of scholarships and turned down for nearly every job he applied for after graduation. In many of his early roles, he felt like an impostor – one bad day away from being exposed.

His nonprofit stumbled through its first year, and at Compass, he came close to being pushed out of the company he had built himself. What kept him moving forward wasn't extraordinary talent or confidence. It was a willingness to learn – from failure, from mentors, and from the people around him – and to keep dreaming bigger every time he got knocked down. In this Blink, you'll discover how Reffkin's upbringing shaped the way he sees the world, why he's convinced that nobody succeeds alone, and how eight principles drawn from his life can help you think bigger, move faster, and bounce back when setbacks inevitably come your way.

#### Chapter 1 of 6

## A fractured start and a mother who built everything anyway

When Ruth, an Israeli immigrant, called her parents after the birth of her son with an African American man from Louisiana, they had just one question. "What is he? " "He is Jewish," Ruth replied, "and Black. " They hung up the phone and disowned them both.

From that day until their deaths, Reffkin never met his maternal grandparents and never exchanged a single word with them. His father, meanwhile, left when he was still a baby. From the very beginning, the world had sent a clear message: he just didn’t belong. But Ruth gave him a different message. Whatever anyone else thought, the two of them had each other. When Reffkin was with his mother, he felt loved, supported, and secure.

As he got older, that feeling of not fitting in only deepened. He was the only Black kid at his synagogue, but he didn’t sit easily with the Black kids at school either – he was mixed, and Jewish. Teachers singled him out. Administrators came down hard on him. The more out of place he felt, the more he craved something real: a genuine sense of belonging in the wider world. That craving shaped much of his life.

It pushed him to New York City. It fueled his ambition. And years later, it inspired the mission behind Compass: helping people find their place in the world. To understand where that drive came from, though, you have to understand Ruth. Ruth grew up under a strict, authoritarian father who saw her independence and curiosity as a problem to be stamped out. When she showed an interest in building a radio as a young girl, he raged at her for pursuing what he considered a boy’s passion.

He punished her for her relationships, refused to support her marriage, and told her she’d end up in the gutter when she wanted to move to California. She went anyway. In California, Ruth built a preschool, managed teams, started businesses, and reinvented herself again and again. She raised Reffkin alone, without family support or financial safety nets, and when she saw he needed more than she could provide on her own, she tracked down every nonprofit, mentor, and scholarship program she could find to give him the chances she couldn’t afford herself.

Today, in her seventies, she's a real estate agent at Compass – still building, still working, still finding ways to create opportunity. To Reffkin, his mother embodies what entrepreneurship really means: resourcefulness, resilience, and a refusal to accept that something can't be done. She gave him more than a childhood. She gave him a blueprint for how to build a life.

#### Chapter 2 of 6

## Mentors help open doors and principles show you through them

Reffkin is quick to point out that he didn't get where he is on his own. Growing up without a father, he spent much of his life looking for guidance. Over time, that search led him to build deep relationships with mentors – relationships that would shape both his career and his view of the world. None of those mentors loomed larger than Vernon Jordan – civil rights leader, presidential advisor, and one of the most powerful figures in both business and politics.

For Reffkin, Jordan embodied something he desperately needed to see: that a Black man could be powerful and respected across every field he entered. When Reffkin told him he was scared to leave Goldman Sachs to start Compass, Jordan told him simply: “Son, opportunity is never convenient. ” Then there was Bayo Ogunlesi, the private equity founder who became Compass’s first investor. When Reffkin went to him looking for a job, Ogunlesi saw something Reffkin couldn’t yet see in himself. He offered $500,000 in backing and a safety net: if Compass failed, he’d hire Reffkin within a year. In that single conversation, he removed all the risk – and Compass was born.

Over those years, Reffkin worked out a clear approach to mentorship that he believes anyone can use. First, bring something to every meeting – an insight or perspective the mentor wouldn’t otherwise have. Second, use the advice you’re given, then report back to show it mattered. And third, don’t limit the conversation to career questions – ask about life, marriage, balance.

Reffkin has found that mentors almost always prefer those conversations anyway. From everything he has learned – from his mother, his mentors, and his own hard experience – Reffkin has distilled eight principles of entrepreneurship. Each one is harder to live by, and more powerful when you do, than it might first appear. Over the next four sections, we’ll look at all eight.

#### Chapter 3 of 6

## Dream without limits and move fast

When the letter from Columbia University arrived, Reffkin sat down with his mother and they both cried. A C-average kid from Berkeley, whose own guidance counselor had told him not to waste the application fee, had gotten in. That moment – the letter in his hands, his mother beside him – was what dreaming big actually looks like when it works. This is the heart of Reffkin’s first principle, Dream Big.

Your ambition is the cap on your potential. Big dreams don’t just give you something to aim for – they generate the energy you need to get there. They stir passion, pull people together, and make it possible to take risks that smaller thinking would never justify. When you genuinely believe something is possible, you find ways to make it happen that simply wouldn’t occur to you otherwise. A practical tool for keeping your thinking honest is what Reffkin calls the “rocking chair test. ” When a decision feels complicated, imagine yourself decades from now, looking back.

From that distance, which choice would you be proud of? Which would you regret? Most things that feel enormous in the moment barely register from that vantage point. The ones that do are the ones where you either spoke up or stayed silent when it mattered. That test was behind some of Reffkin’s most public decisions – including speaking out on racial justice after the murder of George Floyd in 2020. For years he’d followed the advice of his mentors: work twice as hard, keep your head down, hold the doors open for those coming after you.

But watching a younger generation of activists who simply expected to be included taught him that he’d been setting the ceiling of his own dreams too low. Sometimes dreaming big means expanding what you think is even worth dreaming about. Reffkin’s second principle is Move Fast. Speed is really about learning – the faster you move, the faster you find out what works and what doesn’t. Nothing is real until an idea meets the world, and the world’s reaction, positive or negative, is the only feedback that counts. So before you start any project, ask yourself what measurable success actually looks like – not whether the work got done, but whether it changed anything.

The trap most people fall into is measuring effort rather than impact, and feeling good about being busy when nothing has actually moved forward. Look for activities that naturally serve multiple purposes at once – this is very different from multitasking, where your attention is divided and everything suffers. Running 50 marathons across all 50 states was a single commitment for Reffkin that delivered fitness, fundraising, travel, and time with his mother simultaneously, because he was fully present for one thing that happened to serve many. And remember to build in moments to acknowledge what you’ve achieved. Moving fast without ever pausing to take stock is how good momentum quietly turns into burnout.

#### Chapter 4 of 6

## Let reality teach you then drive toward solutions

When Reffkin was DJing parties in high school, he learned an important lesson: the crowd was always right. If the dance floor emptied, he changed the music. If a song got people moving, he played more like it. By paying attention to what was actually happening in front of him – and adjusting accordingly – he became the most in-demand DJ on the Bay Area private school circuit for two years straight.

This is Reffkin’s third principle, Learn from Reality, in action. The fastest way to improve at almost anything is to pay attention to what works, test new ideas quickly, and stay open to the feedback you get. That requires humility – the willingness to recognize good ideas, even when they aren't your own. Take the iPod. Apple didn't invent portable music players, hard drives, or downloadable songs. What it did was combine existing ideas in a way that served customers better than anything that came before.

That's what learning from reality looks like: paying attention to what people want and building from there. The same principle applies in everyday interactions. Watch people's faces when you're speaking. Are they engaged? Confused? Distracted?

Ask yourself: Is this person actually connecting with what I'm saying? If not, don't keep pushing harder. Adjust. Reality is giving you information. Reffkin's fourth principle, Be Solutions Driven, starts with a simple belief: most problems have a solution if you're willing to keep looking for it. He learned that lesson firsthand when scaffolding went up outside Compass's New York headquarters.

The construction covered the company's signage, making the building nearly invisible from the street. Worse, it looked like the scaffolding would stay there for more than a year. The team explored every option they could think of. The city wouldn't budge. The contractor wasn't interested. The construction schedule was fixed.

Eventually, everyone concluded there was nothing they could do. Everyone except Reffkin. Reffkin asked Rory Golod, president of Compass Tri-State, to take another look. Within 72 hours, Golod had found a legal clause allowing businesses to add branding when external construction was causing them harm. A giant Compass banner was designed, printed, and installed over the weekend – and flew in front of the building for over a year and a half, at no cost. When a problem feels impossible, Reffkin suggests asking yourself two questions: If I absolutely had to solve this, how would I do it?

And if this were my only responsibility today, where would I start? Often, breakthrough ideas appear the moment you stop treating obstacles as proof that something can't be done and start treating them as problems waiting to be solved. During his White House Fellowship, Reffkin attended a dinner where he found himself sitting next to Ori Allon, a young Israeli technologist who had recently sold a search algorithm to Google. The two hit it off.

#### Chapter 5 of 6

## Be obsessed by opportunity and leave your ego behind

They exchanged business cards, said their goodbyes, and went back to their lives. For most people, that would have been the end of the story. But Reffkin followed up with a handwritten thank-you note. Then a holiday card.

Then another the following year. He sent birthday messages, checked in from time to time, and stayed in touch without asking for anything in return. Over the next six years, Allon left Google, founded another company, and sold it to Twitter. And when Reffkin was finally ready to launch Compass, Allon was the first person he called. Those years of small, consistent effort had created something valuable: trust. That experience became the foundation of Reffkin's fifth principle, Obsess About Opportunity.

His point isn't that every relationship should be transactional. It's almost the opposite. The best opportunities often come from relationships you've invested in long before you need anything from them. Most people underestimate the power of following up, staying connected, and showing genuine interest in others over time. But those small actions compound. Reffkin believes that caring more than seems necessary, staying in touch longer than feels useful, and investing in relationships without expecting an immediate return can become a powerful competitive advantage.

His sixth principle, Collaborate Without Ego, begins with a different lesson: your values aren't defined by what you say. They're defined by what you're willing to do when there's a cost attached. Early in Compass's history, one of the company's highest-producing agents was generating significant revenue. He was also, according to multiple reports, consistently cruel to the people who worked for him. Firing him was financially risky – other top agents might follow, and the industry might question Compass rather than the agent. Reffkin dragged his feet longer than he should have, then made the call anyway.

He fired him. By Monday, word had spread across the company. People said Compass had become a different kind of place. And the culture that firing helped build attracted far more top talent than that one agent ever could have generated alone. Strong collaboration also requires clarity about responsibility. To create that clarity, Reffkin introduced a concept called Single Point of Accountability.

The idea is simple: every collaborative project should have one person who owns it. That person is responsible for moving it forward, removing obstacles, and answering for its progress. When someone asks for an update, there's one person who can provide it. When a project stalls, there's one person whose job it is to get it moving again. Because when everyone is responsible, nobody really is. And sometimes, all it takes to make a team more effective is making ownership unmistakably clear.

#### Chapter 6 of 6

## Play to your strengths and bounce back when you’re down

Early in his career at McKinsey, Reffkin spent a lot of energy trying to become someone he wasn't. He admired a colleague who excelled at spreadsheets and financial modeling, so he tried to do the same. The harder he pushed, the more frustrated he became. His performance review cut through it.

His manager told him plainly that he was never going to be a great analyst, but that he had real strengths worth focusing on. The advice was simple: acknowledge your weaknesses, find people who cover them, and pour your energy into what you’re actually great at. This is Reffkin’s seventh principle, Maximize Your Strengths. The school system trains us to obsess over what we’re bad at – to bring up the C grade rather than deepen the A. In the real world, the best performers think differently. Build a team whose strengths cover your gaps, and spend your own time doing the things that make you come alive.

It took Reffkin nearly a decade to fully embrace that idea. At Compass, he finally did. He focused on what energized him most: building relationships, inspiring people, and connecting with customers. For everything else, especially financial analysis, he hired people who were better than he was. His eighth and final principle is Bounce Back with Passion. For Reffkin, resilience isn't just about grit.

It's about giving yourself a reason to keep moving. He learned that lesson the hard way during the dot-com crash. His entire life savings – $100,000 that had briefly grown to nearly $1 million – disappeared. What did he do? He called his mother. He talked to a therapist.

He slept. The next morning, he started dreaming again. He mapped out a new plan: attend business school, rebuild his finances through investment banking, and eventually start a nonprofit that could help others. Having a new goal gave him something to move toward instead of dwelling on what he'd lost. His greatest test came years later at Compass. The company had launched as a rental platform, but within a year Reffkin could see it wasn’t working for customers.

He wanted to rebuild entirely around experienced real estate agents – a fundamental change most of the leadership team opposed. While he was making his case internally, the team held a secret breakfast without him. When his cofounder Ori Allon arrived, he found every company leader there – except Reffkin. The message was clear: Reffkin had to go. Shaken, Reffkin went home and talked with his wife, Benís, who encouraged him not to give up. Later that evening, Allon asked him two simple questions.

If Compass focused entirely on helping agents succeed, given their ability to attract talent and raise money, wouldn't they eventually win? And, if you're being chased by a bear, do you need to outrun the bear – or just the person next to you? Who, he asked, was running beside Compass? The answer was traditional real estate brokerages – companies that were much slower to adapt. So Reffkin stayed the course. He persuaded the team, Compass changed direction, and the company was rebuilt on a far stronger foundation.

Looking back, Reffkin sees the same lesson in every setback he faced. Success doesn't belong to the people who never fail. It belongs to the people who keep finding a reason to begin again.

#### Conclusion

## Final summary

The main takeaway of this Blink to No One Succeeds Alone by Robert Reffkin is that success is never a solo achievement. Behind every accomplishment are the people who believed in us, challenged us, opened doors for us, and helped us become who we are. As Reffkin's story shows, mentors – and in his case, especially his mother – often deserve just as much credit as we do. Along the way, Reffkin developed eight principles that shaped both his life and his career.

Dream Big and Move Fast. Learn from Reality and Be Solutions Driven. Obsess About Opportunity and Collaborate Without Ego. Maximize Your Strengths. And when setbacks inevitably come, Bounce Back with Passion. Taken together, these principles all point to the same idea.

Success isn't about having the perfect background, avoiding failure, or doing everything yourself. It's about staying curious, building strong relationships, learning from every experience, and finding the courage to begin again whenever life knocks you down. Reffkin's story is a reminder that where you start doesn't determine where you'll end up. What matters is who you learn from, how boldly you dream, and whether you're willing to get back up one more time. Okay, that’s it for this Blink.

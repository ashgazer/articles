#### Introduction

## What’s in it for me? Understand why you believe what you do –⁠ and why others don’t.

Most of us assume our opinions are the product of reason, with a dash of influence from our upbringing. We weigh the evidence, absorb the influence of our parents and peers, and arrive at our conclusions – or so we think. That story is partly true, but it leaves out something stranger and more unsettling. Did you know, for example, that your political views can be predicted from the density of taste buds on your tongue, the symmetry of your face, the tidiness of your bedroom, and even whether you put pineapple on pizza?

My name is Tom and in this Blink, I’ll take you on a journey through biology, geography, history, and psychology to uncover the hidden forces secretly shaping what you think. You’ll be asked to consider what it means that your beliefs are predictable and why that matters. And finally, you’ll learn why being willing to see both sides is so important –⁠ and why the surprising antidote to increasing polarization isn’t agreement, but continuing to disagree.

#### Chapter 1 of 5

## The political climate

Imagine saying the word “twelfths” out loud across a windy field, trying to convey a message to your fellow farmer. You probably don’t have a field to try this, or a farmer at hand, but let me tell you, this task is quite a struggle – especially if you live in more northern latitudes. The way humans talk is strongly influenced by the conditions where they live. Near the equator, where people spend much of their time outdoors, languages lean on big, booming vowels that carry across distances.

Just think of the rolling sounds of Hausa, Arabic, or Italian. Now, head north toward the Arctic, where freezing weather drives conversation indoors, close to the fire. Those big vowels tighten into pinched umlauts and clattering consonants. In other words, climate sculpts the words in our mouths. And it also sculpts something far deeper: our beliefs. Hot, humid places are paradises for bacteria, viruses, and parasites, where food rots and infections spread rapidly.

To survive this, humans evolved two defenses. The first is biological, the immune system. The second is behavioral; it’s the customs and beliefs we adopt to avoid catching things, from not kissing strangers on the mouth to throwing out spoiled food. Many of those immune-protective behaviors take the form of attitudes. And those attitudes cluster into something resembling social conservatism. For example, societies in pathogen-rich environments tend to enforce strict rules around hygiene and sex.

And they exhibit xenophobic tendencies – they distrust foreigners who might be carrying disease. Just compare the patriarchal, hierarchical Azande people who live in hot, humid Central Africa with the egalitarian, sexually relaxed! Kung of the bone-dry, low-pathogen Kalahari. Or the cold, dry Netherlands, which accepts drugs and prostitution, while tropical Singapore canes people for soliciting sex and outlawed homosexuality until 2022. The same logic extends beyond disease to every kind of environmental threat. Places battered by extreme weather of any kind, such as Yemen, Russia, Turkey, and Somalia, tend to lean conservative and embrace strong, authoritarian leadership.

Harsh conditions demand the kind of tight social order and cohesion that loose, egalitarian arrangements can’t provide. Surprisingly, our gods, too, seem to follow this map. In temperate places like Greece and Rome, we find spoiled, amoral gods who care little about human behavior. Then there’s the other kind –⁠ the all-seeing, all-judging, vengeful gods obsessed with how their followers behave.

These ethical religions, with their commandments and threats of damnation, tend to emerge in harsh, disaster-prone regions, especially places gripped by drought –⁠ the deadliest climatic killer of all. It’s no accident that the parched Middle East gave birth to Judaism, Christianity, and Islam. When catastrophe threatens to shatter a community, strict moral codes that compel cooperation and solidarity become a kind of behavioral immunity against social collapse itself.

#### Chapter 2 of 5

## Granite versus limestone

Across the Deep South of the US, a region almost entirely Republican since 1960, there runs a strange, bright blue, Democratic arc curving from the Arkansas border through Alabama and Georgia and up into Virginia. Looking at a political map, an earth scientist named Steven Dutch recognized that swoosh instantly. It followed, almost exactly, a band of dark, rich, alkaline soil termed “the Black Belt. ” The Black Belt was the richest cotton land in the South.

And that’s precisely why enslaved people were brought there in the greatest numbers. Today, the descendants of those enslaved workers still populate those counties, and they vote Democrat. As Dutch put it, soils make agriculture, agriculture makes economies, and economies make voting patterns. France offers an eerily similar story. In 1910, a failed politician named André Siegfried overheard a local saying in the Vendée region of France: “Granite gives us priests, limestone gives us teachers. ” Studying decades of voting records, André Siegfried identified a north-south political split between conservative-leaning monarchists and left-leaning revolutionaries.

When he laid an electoral map over a geological one, the two matched almost perfectly. Granite voted right; limestone voted left. Why is this? Put simply, water. Granite, which dominates in the north, drains well and supports rich farmland. This concentrated power in the hands of landed gentry who built churches and hired priests.

Limestone, which dominates in the south, is porous and drains poorly, leaving behind nutrient-poor soil. Unlike in the north, the south could only support small, clustered hamlets of self-sufficient farmers, bound by shared labor and primed against authority. The physical environments we inhabit shape how we live, how we earn our bread, and ultimately how we think and vote. This logic may even explain one of the grandest divides of all: between the individualist West and the collectivist East. Take ancient Greece, a network of rocky islands and arid hills where people survived by herding, fishing, and trading wine and oil. These were all tasks a single person or family could manage alone.

Now compare the fertile but waterlogged plains of central China, where the one viable crop was rice. Rice farming takes a village –⁠ even a whole county. It demands elaborate irrigation systems built and maintained through the cooperation of dozens or hundreds of smallholders. A people whose survival depended on collaboration would naturally come to value harmony, interdependence, and the group. And a people who could thrive alone could afford to prize the individual. So it may not have been Aristotle or Confucius who split the world into two ways of seeing –⁠ but instead, the geography into which they were born.

#### Chapter 3 of 5

## How our faces shape our minds

A toy piece of facial-recognition software, downloaded free from the internet, can look at your profile picture and guess whether you’re liberal or conservative about 72 percent of the time. That figure held across more than a million faces from the US, UK, and Canada –⁠ even when obvious clues like age, race, and gender were stripped away. Humans, by comparison, guess correctly only 55 percent of the time. In short, our faces can reveal the contents of our minds.

But even more startlingly, our faces can actually shape our minds. Take what teenagers call “pretty privilege. ” Multiple large studies show that conventionally attractive people tend to lean conservative. The reason is disarmingly simple, it’s because beautiful people live in a kinder world. From infancy, babies with more symmetrical faces get more eye contact and warmth. Attractive students get better grades, attractive employees earn more, and even attractive criminals receive lighter sentences.

Growing up surrounded by people who assume the best of you makes the world look like a fair place that rewards merit. As a result, beautiful people tend to believe more strongly in meritocracy, value individualism, and feel less urgency about inequality they’ve rarely suffered. But the arrangement inside the skull matters even more. Researchers at the University of Nebraska tracked students’ eyes as they viewed collages mixing pleasant images, like a chocolate-dipped strawberry, with nasty ones, like a spider on a face. Everyone glanced at the threat first –⁠ that’s basic evolutionary wiring. But conservatives lingered on the unpleasant image noticeably longer and kept returning to it, while liberals gravitated back to the strawberry.

Being hyper-attuned to danger seems to predict conservative leanings. Conservatism can even be predicted by looking in your mouth. “Supertasters,” who have more taste buds and thus experience flavors more intensely, tend to vote right. Heightened sensitivity to strong taste is just another form of vigilance against potential threat. This relates to what psychologists call disgust sensitivity –⁠ basically, how strongly you react to gross things. People whose brains light up harder at images of vomit, maggots, or wormy food tend to lean conservative.

Strong sensitivity to physical disgust predicts strong moral disgust. For some people, contemplating blasphemy or a desecrated flag triggers the same bodily reaction as stepping in dog poop –⁠ all the reflexes that evolved to shield us from contagion. Twin studies suggest genetics explains a whopping 60 percent of the variance in our general left-right outlook. Essentially, though, biology doesn’t determine our politics –⁠ it just nudges us.

The right formula is something like “nature via nurture. ” Picture a bucket half-filled with genetic predisposition. Life experiences drop in pebbles that may or may not make it overflow.

#### Chapter 4 of 5

## We think what we feel

In the summer of 2024, Britain erupted in violence. Mosques were attacked, hotels were set ablaze, and over a hundred police officers were injured. The riots ran from late July to mid-August, almost exactly matching the hottest ten days of the year. This wasn’t a fluke.

British rioting tends to happen roughly once a decade, and the dates skew overwhelmingly toward summer. History tells the same story worldwide. The French Revolution began in July, the St. Bartholomew’s Day massacre in August, and the Stonewall riots in June. Civil unrest rises with the temperature, peaking somewhere between 27 and 32 degrees Celsius. Above that, it’s simply too hot to bother.

Heat may not invent our opinions, but it can certainly inflame them. How we feel powerfully shapes what we think. For centuries, Western thought has tried to wall feeling off from reason, treating emotion as irrational and thinking as logical. But that split is a fiction. The neuroscientist Antonio Damasio proved this through a patient he called “Elliot,” a successful accountant who lost part of his frontal lobe to a tumor. Elliot’s IQ stayed in the top few percent, but he could no longer feel.

Without emotion to guide him, Elliot made catastrophic decisions; he lost all of his savings, his jobs, and his marriages. The lesson is profound, we cannot reason well without feeling. But each emotion bends our thinking in its own direction. Fear makes us pessimistic and risk-averse. People primed with frightening news wildly overestimate how many people die from various causes. Anger does the opposite, filling us with reckless optimism and “tunnel vision” that fixates us on the target of our rage while blinding us to the consequences.

And anxiety breeds pessimism and pushes us to jump to conclusions, which is partly why it correlates with conspiracy thinking. The emotion looming largest over today’s politics, though, is loneliness. It’s now a global epidemic, and a century of research links it to hostility. Chronically lonely people have their empathy circuits tuned out, processing others’ suffering not as a call for compassion but as a personal threat. Isolation makes us more aggressive, too. Studies have linked social isolation to support for extreme parties across Europe and to fervent political movements in the US.

These moods ripple across whole societies. Suicide rates jumped after the 2008 financial crash, and the pandemic produced tens of millions of new cases of depression and anxiety. Fear, anger, and isolation all push us in the same dangerous direction –⁠ toward distrust and zero-sum thinking. Since politics grow from genuine feelings, you can’t just argue people out of them with logic. To change minds, we often have to change the underlying emotions first –⁠ by tackling loneliness, insecurity, and hopelessness, and by cultivating the happiness, safety, and awe that make us curious, generous, and open.

#### Chapter 5 of 5

## Why we should keep arguing

Biologist Lee Dugatkin once conducted an experiment involving a school of guppies. He sorted the little fish into high, medium, and low wariness groups and then introduced them to a pumpkinseed –⁠ a fish with a particular taste for guppies. Quickly, a grim pattern emerged. The bold, curious guppies were nearly all eaten within thirty-six hours, while the anxious, wary ones huddled at the bottom and survived.

If caution is so clearly better, why do reckless guppies exist at all? The answer is habitat. Downstream, among predators, wariness keeps you alive. But upstream, in predator-free waters, curiosity lets you feast, mate, and explore while your nervous cousins waste their lives in hiding. In other words, each trait⁠ thrives in its own environment. This humble fish may be the best lens we have for understanding human politics.

Scientists working in the new field of biopolitics argue that left-wing and right-wing attitudes are also natural expressions of our genes that evolved in response to different levels of threat. Conservatism looks like the political equivalent of dodging the pumpkinseed: order, hierarchy, and wariness of outsiders. And liberalism looks like the strategy of the safe upstream shallows: curiosity, inclusion, and risk-taking. Ultimately, neither type is universally better, which is precisely why neither has won out. A society with a full spectrum of dispositions is more adaptive than one with only a single setting –⁠ we need both, because we face both peace and war. If no one can ever win, then what’s the point of arguing?

Well, because reasoning may be less an individual sport than a team one. Just take the famous puzzle called the Wason four-card selection. Most people –⁠ even very intelligent ones –⁠ are remarkably bad at it, getting the answer wrong 85 percent of the time. But remarkably, that number flips when people work together. In small groups, people get the puzzle right 80 percent of the time. Seen this way, no one person can hold all of the truth –⁠ and the biases that cripple us alone become powerful when we argue together.

Your stubbornness forces me to come up with good reasons to convince you, and mine does the same for you. Truth emerges in the back-and-forth, like a dance. The philosopher Isaiah Berlin called the belief in a single correct answer monism and warned it was the seed of totalitarianism. The wiser path, he declared, is pluralism –⁠ accepting that values like liberty and equality genuinely conflict and can’t be resolved by reason alone.

So the real battle today isn’t left versus right. It’s between those who treat their opponents as partners and those who seek to eliminate them. We must love our political neighbors, because argument is how we evolved to think. The main takeaway of this Blink to Why We Think What We Think by Turi Munthe is that… The opinions we hold most dear –⁠ about politics, religion, morality, and more –⁠ are far less the product of pure reason than we imagine, and far more the result of hidden forces like climate, geography, our faces and brains, our genes, and our shifting emotions.

#### Conclusion

## Final summary

Because left and right evolved as adaptive responses to different environments, neither side is simply “right” or “wrong” –⁠ both bring survival-critical strengths, and a society needs the full spectrum to thrive. The deepest lesson is that disagreement isn’t a flaw to be eliminated but the very engine of good thinking.

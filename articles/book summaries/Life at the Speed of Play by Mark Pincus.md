#### Introduction

## What’s in it for me? Test ideas faster, build products people love, and scale without losing judgment.

Most ideas never become anything. Some are abandoned too early, while others consume months of effort before anyone discovers they were never worth building. Today, creating is faster than ever. New tools can turn a rough concept into a working prototype in days, sometimes hours.

That speed is powerful, but it also raises the stakes: when you can pursue more possibilities, choosing the right ones becomes even more important. Progress depends on knowing what deserves commitment, what should be tested quickly, and what needs to be discarded without regret. In this Blink, you’ll learn how to make sharper decisions under uncertainty, turn promising convictions into products people genuinely value, and keep strong judgment alive as ideas, teams, and opportunities grow. The process begins before any product is built, with a simple question about the direction of your own life.

#### Chapter 1 of 8

## A clear direction keeps your life from drifting

What if you reached the end of the year and realized that, despite staying busy, you’d made little progress toward the life you actually wanted? Daily demands can fill your calendar while larger ambitions remain postponed. Regular reflection helps you notice that drift and choose your direction more deliberately. One practice for taking control is to create a *Book of Life*, a private record you revisit each year.

Describe your current situation honestly. What feels satisfying? What remains unresolved? Where is your energy going? Then review the previous year against the promises you made to yourself. Older entries reveal recurring goals, neglected problems, and years that blur together because little changed.

Let one question guide the exercise: What could you do now that your future self would appreciate? Combine one commitment you can control, such as improving a habit or addressing a health concern, with a larger ambition whose outcome is uncertain. The first builds confidence in your ability to shape your behavior. The second gives the year lasting significance. Another useful concept is the *Imagination Chessboard*. Picture where you want to be two or five years from now, then write that future as if it already exists.

Suppose you want to build ambitious products but believe you first need $500 million and a permanent startup incubator. Working backward may reveal that these are assumptions, and that you can begin using more of your creative capacity now. The exercise exposes hidden limits and helps you identify the next meaningful move. Once your destination is clearer, the next challenge is deciding which beliefs deserve commitment and which ideas should remain replaceable.

#### Chapter 2 of 8

## Strong instincts survive weak ideas

A promising product can fail because its creators become loyal to the first solution they imagined. Praise, funding, or temporary growth may keep that solution alive even when users show little enthusiasm. Progress begins by separating the belief behind an idea from the idea itself. It’s useful to distinguish between an instinct and an idea.

An instinct is a lasting belief about something people need, while an idea is one possible way to meet that need. For instance, you might correctly sense that hailing a taxi by phone call is slow and inconvenient. Your first idea could be to let passengers text the existing dispatcher, keeping the taxi system largely unchanged. That solution may fail, even though the underlying instinct remains sound. Uber addressed this need through smartphone apps, live maps, and a much larger network of available drivers. The lesson is that evidence against one implementation should prompt you to replace the idea, rather than automatically abandon the human need behind it.

Capable entrepreneurs may be right about their instincts around 95 percent of the time, while any particular idea succeeds no more than about 25 percent of the time. Always be on the lookout for *instinct veins* -- recurring beliefs about human behavior that can generate several product ideas. Suppose you believe technology should feel simple and dependable. Your first idea might be a service that provides customers with a free computer and manages their internet experience. Interviews could reveal that customers distrust the offer and worry about replacing a machine that already holds their software and files. Rather than abandon the underlying belief, you could drop that idea and explore another way to reduce technical frustration, such as software that diagnoses and fixes computer problems.

The instinct stays steady while evidence guides you toward a stronger solution. Hold firmly to the human need you’ve identified, and let evidence decide how it should be served. Next, you’ll see how to test an idea before investing heavily in building it.

#### Chapter 3 of 8

## The smallest test can prevent the largest waste

A team can spend months polishing an early release, only to discover that users never wanted the underlying idea. By then, money has been spent and abandoning the work feels harder. The usual safeguard is a *Minimum Viable Product*, an early version that people can use in real situations. In practice, the word viable often encourages teams to keep building until the product feels respectable.

That delay undermines fast learning when the first release is unlikely to become the winner. A more useful concept is the *Minimum Idea State*. This is the smallest expression of an idea that can produce meaningful directional feedback. It could be a text link or a rough digital demo that works only briefly. Its purpose is to reveal whether real interest exists before substantial development begins. Zynga wanted to know whether *Mafia Wars* players might also be interested in its poker game.

Instead of building a full crossover feature, the team briefly added a link inviting players to rob opponents at the poker tables, even though the destination hadn’t yet been created. In two separate one-minute tests, 22. 7 percent of players clicked. That strong response gave the team enough confidence to spend 24 hours building themed poker tables, which helped bring one million new installations to *Zynga Poker* within a week. This shows the value of testing the smallest possible version of an idea before investing heavily in it. Raw tests lower the cost of each attempt, allowing teams to try more ideas before committing.

You can go further by gathering evidence before proposing a solution. Ask potential users how they spend their time and what repeatedly frustrates them, then let their behavior shape what you build. The goal is conviction grounded in evidence. Once demand appears, the next question is which parts should follow proven patterns and where novelty can earn its place.

#### Chapter 4 of 8

## Strong products combine familiar value with focused novelty

Originality can become a liability when every part of a product asks users to learn something unfamiliar. A safer route begins with an experience the audience already understands. Innovation can then be concentrated where it has the greatest chance of creating real excitement. A practical framework for doing this is *Proven Better New.*

Start with *Proven*, meaning a product already valued by the same audience you hope to serve. Study it closely enough to understand which mechanics produce that value, then reuse those elements legally and accurately. Rebuilding familiar basics wastes development time and can generate misleading feedback when they’re executed poorly. Next comes *Better*. Choose an improvement that existing users would immediately recognize as meaningfully superior. Teams often confuse their own preferences with customer value, so the intended audience must confirm the improvement.

A change that sounds appealing but lacks strong evidence belongs in the riskier category. That category is *New*, the untested feature that gives the product a reason to attract attention. Since novelty is where failure is most likely, isolate it and test several versions instead of making the whole experience experimental. *Zynga Poker* followed this structure in 2007.

It retained familiar online-poker rules and table design, removed the required download, and introduced real identities and friends at the table. This framework helps teams avoid making novelty the goal in itself. The strongest opportunities often come from taking something users already value and improving one part of it so clearly that the market becomes worth revisiting. Once those risks are separated, the next step is turning them into a roadmap that guides what gets built.

#### Chapter 5 of 8

## A living roadmap turns strategy into measurable work

A brilliant strategy can still disappear inside a crowded backlog. Once teams begin building, priorities blur, estimates expand, and everyone stays busy without knowing whether the work is moving the product forward. A living roadmap prevents that drift by tying each development cycle to a clear result. A useful practice is *roadmapping*, a weekly operating system for deciding what gets built, why it matters, how much effort it deserves, and when the evidence will be reviewed.

The roadmap changes as new information arrives. Each proposed feature starts with a hypothesis linked to an objective, along with its expected effect and required engineering time. After release, actual user behavior is compared with the forecast, and the result shapes the next set of bets. At Zynga, teams made this discipline concrete by recording each feature’s expected impact and engineering cost, then checking those predictions against real results. This exposed low-value tweaks and helped direct about 80 percent of development time toward features users might value, compared with an estimated 20 percent at a strong competitor. That made the same engineering capacity roughly four times more productive.

This weekly review also turns one team’s discovery into shared knowledge. When *FarmVille* found that players who helped neighbors water crops were retained at three times the usual rate, other game teams began testing related social features within days. Successful ideas spread quickly, while disappointing results became useful evidence. Roadmapping keeps commitments visible and forces predictions to face reality. With that system in place, the next challenge is choosing bets ambitious enough to produce a major leap.

#### Chapter 6 of 8

## Bold experiments can create meaningful leaps

Product teams often lose momentum in two familiar ways. They spend weeks polishing tiny improvements that barely affect users, or commit to enormous projects that delay feedback for months. The answer is to turn a large ambition into a focused experiment. This kind of experiment is called a *Bold Beat*.

It tests one substantial change that could meaningfully alter user behavior, while remaining quick enough to build, measure, and remove. At Zynga, a Bold Beat had to reach at least 25 percent of users and improve a key measure by 10 percent or more. Speed matters because Bold Beats are built for learning. Teams can release a rough version, watch behavior closely, and invest in a polished system after genuine demand appears. *FarmVille* tested crafting through a quickly assembled feature that let players combine crops into useful goods. Strong engagement justified six months of deeper development, and crafting became the game’s most durable engagement driver and a foundation for *FarmVille 2*.

A Bold Beat can also reveal an entirely new direction. An engineer spent half a day making FarmVille’s animals appear to move with alternating images. Players began imagining breeding and other interactive possibilities, showing that decorative objects could become new areas of play. When one of these experiments keeps working, it can become a repeatable pattern that drives future growth. The aim is to make bold ideas testable before they become expensive. Next, you’ll see how to preserve that speed and product judgment as an organization grows.

#### Chapter 7 of 8

## Product quality must lead every attempt to scale

Growth can quietly damage the product that created it. As teams expand, leaders spend more time managing the organization, while decisions about the user experience move further from those who understand it best. The first responsibility remains getting the product right. A useful principle is to stay close to the metal.

This means using the product yourself, studying how customers behave, and noticing details that broad metrics may miss. That direct contact sharpens your judgment about what makes an experience feel clear, useful, and satisfying. Testing those judgments with representative users then shows whether your sense of quality is accurate. A practical method for building that contact is *Humanize Before You Productize*. Deliver an experience manually before automating it, so you can observe what customers value and fix problems immediately. Airbnb’s founders applied this approach by visiting their 30 New York hosts, taking better photographs, discussing prices, and hearing complaints firsthand.

After they improved a faulty calendar, the host count doubled to 60 the following week and continued growing. Eventually, direct involvement must extend through other leaders. Scaling begins when a team can preserve sound product judgment without constant supervision. Give employees meaningful ownership, let promising contributors operate above their current level, and develop leaders inside the company where they can learn its users and standards. Shared values should emerge from real product decisions and help everyone recognize what good work looks like. Growth also creates distractions, from publicity to investor demands.

A leader’s calendar should therefore reveal their priorities. At least half of their time should remain connected to product work, with an ideal target closer to 80 percent. With product judgment protected inside a growing organization, the final challenge is applying it on new platforms where the rules are still being written.

#### Chapter 8 of 8

## New platforms reward those who learn their native possibilities

A new technology rarely arrives with instructions for its most valuable use. Early versions feel awkward, the benefits remain uncertain, and familiar products are often copied into the new environment. The larger opportunities appear after builders understand how the platform changes what users can do. A useful concept for approaching this uncertainty is the *New Planet*.

Each emerging platform develops its own capabilities, habits, and expert communities. Today’s likely New Planets include AI, robotics, space technology, crypto, and the metaverse. Because little has been validated, progress depends on participating early enough to learn directly. Platform shifts tend to produce two waves of successful products. The first wave contains predictable applications that update existing services with the new technology. The second arrives after sustained use reveals experiences that were previously difficult to imagine.

Reaching that stage requires becoming native to the platform by experimenting with its tools, observing early users, and building alongside those already exploring its limits. Learn to work at your curious edges, where an emerging technology meets something you already understand or care about. Small projects can reveal unexpected demand without requiring a complete commitment. When an experiment attracts unusually strong attention, that heat provides a reason to continue. Your chances of finding such signals improve when you broaden your exposure. Spend time with active builders, unusual communities, and specialists who see possibilities beyond mainstream discussion.

Give yourself permission to arrive early and feel uncomfortable. The future becomes clearer through participation, so follow your curiosity and begin testing something this week. The main takeaway of this Blink to *Life at the Speed of Play* by Mark Pincus is that great products emerge from disciplined curiosity, rapid testing, and honest attention to real user behavior.

#### Conclusion

## Final summary

Start by choosing a clear direction, then separate the human need you believe in from any single solution. Test ideas in their smallest useful form, build on what already works, and focus novelty where it can create genuine value. Use living roadmaps and bold experiments to turn strategy into learning, while staying close to the product as the organization grows. On emerging platforms, participate early, follow strong signals, and remain willing to replace weak ideas without abandoning the conviction behind them.

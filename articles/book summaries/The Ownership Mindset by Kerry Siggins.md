#### Introduction

## What’s in it for me? Lead with more heart, honesty and purpose.

What does it take to lead with real purpose, create sustainable success, and inspire people along the way? This Blink follows the story of a CEO whose path to the top wasn’t a straight line – it included a brush with addiction and a recovery that reshaped how she thinks about responsibility. From that experience, she built a leadership philosophy centered on full ownership: not blaming circumstances, but treating every outcome as something you helped create. Whether you’re a seasoned leader or just starting your career, this thoughtful guide will show you leadership starts from within, why honesty is the key to good company culture, and why businesses succeed best when they invest in the people who run them.

#### Chapter 1 of 6

## The paradox of leadership

At the heart of leadership lies a dark paradox. Rising to the top takes drive, but that same hunger to be recognized can build a career and quietly poison it at the same time. The author experienced this herself. As a young, ambitious saleswoman, she spent her twenties chasing recognition at almost any cost, even undermining a struggling colleague to win a sales award – all while hiding a serious drug problem. The colleague got fired; she got the promotion. It felt like winning, until the night she overdosed alone on her apartment floor. Lying there, she thought about every relationship she’d damaged and every person she’d stepped over to be seen as successful. When she finally returned to work, not one coworker had called to check on her. The image she’d built had no substance behind it. That collapse became the foundation for everything that followed. The author didn’t blame anyone for her mistakes. She owned them, then tried her best to fix them. Rebuilding meant leaving the city she worked in, moving in with her mother, and starting over with nothing but a willingness to be honest about her failures. It also meant relearning how to build relationships that weren’t transactional. When a promising job offer at a small manufacturing company went quiet, she swallowed her pride and sent a follow-up email. The silence turned out to be nothing more than a misaddressed message. The job was hers after all. That company ran on a different operating philosophy altogether. Born from a failed mining-tools business that pivoted into industrial cleaning after the nuclear industry collapsed, it had grown into one of the rare organizations owned largely by its own employees. Its culture rested on a simple expectation: every person takes ownership – of their tasks, their attitude, their mistakes, and their impact on others. Leadership shares financial information openly, trusts employees with real autonomy, and treats accountability as something built from the ground up rather than imposed from the top down. This company was StoneAge, the place the author eventually came to lead. And it changed everything she thought she knew about leading.

#### Chapter 2 of 6

## Leadership begins from the inside

Most leadership advice points outward: manage your team, set the vision, drive results. But real leadership starts with a harder task – understanding yourself well enough to know when you’re getting in your own way. That lesson can show up in painfully ordinary moments. One evening, after a brutal week at work, the author climbed onto her exercise bike for an intense workout, hoping to burn off the stress before it boiled over. Her eight-year-old son kept interrupting to ask if he could play a video game. Her patience finally gave out, and what came out of her wasn’t even words – just a burst of pure frustration. When she caught her breath, her son pointed out that yelling like that didn’t actually fix anything. He was right. She hadn’t paused to ask why she felt so on edge, or whether hopping on a bike mid-burnout was a good idea. She’d reacted instead of reflecting, and the fallout landed on someone who didn’t deserve it. Feedback from a coach can sting differently. Told that she leaned on the word “I” constantly in conversation, the author started listening to herself more carefully – and found the habit was everywhere. Digging into where it came from led her somewhere uncomfortable: a long-standing hunger for recognition, rooted in a strained bond with her father growing up. Rather than dismissing the comment as unfair, she sat with it long enough to see the pattern underneath. Both moments point to the same lesson: self-awareness isn’t a personality trait some people happen to have. It’s a skill built through paying attention – to your emotional triggers, unconscious habits, and blind spots – and then having the humility to act on what you find. Personality assessments can help name these patterns, but the real work happens in the in-between moments: catching yourself before you snap, noticing the words you default to, asking why a piece of feedback stung as much as it did. Leaders who skip this inner work tend to repeat the same mistakes under different names. Those who do it lead with more patience, more honesty, and considerably less collateral damage along the way.

#### Chapter 3 of 6

## Vulnerability makes you a better leader

The old image of a leader is someone who never shows doubt, never apologizes, and never lets anyone see them rattled. That image is wrong. The leaders who build real trust are the ones willing to admit when they’ve messed up – and willing to sit with hard feedback instead of brushing it off. Consider what happened after a customer of StoneAge received a damaged, clearly used product that should have been brand new. Furious, the author marched into the shop and confronted her team on the spot, assuming the worst before anyone had a chance to explain. Her sales manager pulled her aside afterward and told her, plainly, that her team was every bit as invested in getting it right for the customer as she was, and that she’d judged them unfairly. Embarrassed, she walked back into the shop and apologized to each person individually. Rebuilding that trust took a long time, but owning the mistake out loud, rather than defending it, was what made repair possible at all. A similar test came from inside her own leadership team. A direct report told her that what she called bold decision-making, one colleague saw as inconsistent and hard to follow. The comment was sharp enough to make her seriously question whether she belonged in the role at all. Rather than dismiss it, she talked it through with her husband and recognized an unflattering truth in it: her constant pursuit of new ideas had started to feel chaotic rather than visionary to the people working under her. That moment of doubt pushed her to communicate with more discipline and focus. Neither story is easy. Both involve a leader admitting fault, sitting with criticism, and changing course because of it. Leadership built on never appearing wrong is fragile, because it can’t survive contact with an actual mistake. Leadership built on honest accountability gets stronger every time it’s tested, because the people around you learn that you’ll own your missteps rather than hide from them. Strength, in other words, isn’t the absence of doubt or error; it’s what you do with them once they show up.

#### Chapter 4 of 6

## Strong leadership is built on honesty

Just as in your personal life, strong work relationships are built on honesty, even when it’s uneasy. This should run both ways between leader and employee. In practice, that means asking questions instead of assuming the worst, raising what’s bothering you directly with the person involved instead of venting to someone else, and offering to help solve the problem rather than just naming it. The alternative – staying quiet, complaining to others, deciding it’s not your job to fix – keeps you stuck and slowly erodes trust. Candor doesn’t need to equate to rudeness. You can be kind without softening your point into vagueness, and stay open to the possibility you’re missing part of the story. Back up feedback with a concrete example rather than a general impression, and once the conversation’s over, ask how it landed – candor is a skill you keep sharpening, not one you master all at once. Transparency works the same way at scale. When StoneAge’s leadership team secretly planned a major business shift behind locked doors, the secrecy alone bred suspicion among employees that took months to repair. The lesson stuck: when a ransomware attack crippled the company’s systems in 2020, the author told employees everything she knew, as she learned it. The result was a company that shipped over a million dollars in orders by hand in a single week, without paying the ransom. Building that kind of trust as a habit, rather than a crisis response, means an open-door policy people actually use, walking the floor instead of waiting for problems to find you, and feedback surveys that aren’t anonymous – fixing a problem is easier when you know where it’s coming from. Even conflict doesn’t have to undermine a team if it’s handled directly: check your own role in the friction first, try to see the other side, and address it calmly once you’re not reacting emotionally. Not every irritation needs to become a confrontation.

#### Chapter 5 of 6

## Good leaders make employees feel seen

People show up differently when their work feels like it fits who they are. The author knows this firsthand – in her early career, she drifted through jobs that didn’t match her skills or interests, and that mismatch fed a much larger personal unraveling. Research from McKinsey found that nearly all surveyed executives felt their work expressed their personal purpose, while only a small fraction of frontline employees said the same. The disparity reflects how rarely purpose gets actively built into day-to-day roles. Daniel Pink’s research on motivation further demonstrates that once people are paid fairly, what actually drives them is autonomy, mastery, and a sense of purpose – not external incentives. Motivating people day to day comes down to a handful of concrete habits: give employees real say over how they get their work done rather than dictating every step; ask more than you tell, since “What do you suggest?” reveals what matters far faster than a directive does; hold regular one-on-ones and treat them as a real exchange, not a box to check; and be transparent about the reasoning behind decisions, since people who understand the “why” stay far more engaged. One of the author’s employees felt boxed in by a role that didn’t match her talents, with no clear next step in sight. Rather than letting her walk out the door, Siggins worked with her to reshape her responsibilities around what she actually did best. Within a month, she was thriving – and she later moved into a senior leadership position. Building this kind of fit deliberately means getting to know employees well enough to understand their actual strengths, using a structured assessment if needed, and staying genuinely open to rewriting a job description rather than treating it as fixed. Consistency is what makes this stick. StoneAge replaced its once-a-year performance reviews with quarterly “Own It Chats,” built around a short set of honest questions covering what’s going well, what isn’t, current priorities, what support is needed, and feedback in return. Underneath all of this is a simple belief: people aren’t looking for perks or constant praise. They’re looking for work that uses what they’re actually good at, in service of something that matters to them, and it’s a leader’s job to help them find that alignment, again and again – not just once.

#### Chapter 6 of 6

## Mature leaders are self-aware

Bold leadership means pairing conviction with clarity about where you’re actually headed. The author learned this the hard way when StoneAge expanded into automated equipment: she applied the company’s old playbook, built for simple handheld tools, to a far more complex product line, and the mismatch cost time, money, and reputation. The fix was a clearer strategy – one that named the specific problem being solved and broke the larger vision into pieces people could act on day to day. With that clarity in place, real risk-taking becomes possible rather than reckless. StoneAge’s acquisition of Breadware, an IoT development partner, closed at brutal timing – just as the pandemic began upending the economy. The author spent sleepless nights weighing whether to proceed, then moved forward anyway, backed by solid due diligence and a strategic rationale worked out well in advance. Within months, the acquired company was outperforming its own targets, and the bet reshaped StoneAge’s broader direction toward technology and data services. Bold moves like this depend on something harder to teach than strategy: self-awareness. Years earlier, a cofounder had told the author plainly that he’d expected more from her leadership. The comment stung, but it landed – she hadn’t set the tone she wanted, and it pushed her to lead more deliberately. This is what constitutes a true leader’s maturity. Rather than suppressing emotions or always staying composed, a mature leader catches their own reactions before they take over a room. The author gets visibly passionate in disagreements, something her team eventually told her could come across as overpowering rather than persuasive. Adopting a personal mantra –”Cool, calm, and collected” – became her way of interrupting that pattern in the moment. Strategy gives a leader direction; bold action moves them forward. But it’s the willingness to look honestly at your own behavior, again and again, that determines whether people actually want to follow. This is what true leadership is all about.

#### Conclusion

## Final summary

In this Blink to The Ownership Mindset you’ve learned what it takes to lead with heart, honesty and purpose. After a personal rock-bottom forced her to rebuild from nothing, the author found her way to a company already run on radical ownership — and eventually rose to lead it herself. Along the way, she learned that real leadership starts by aiming inward: facing your own blind spots, welcoming hard feedback instead of deflecting it, and choosing honesty even when it’s uncomfortable. You’ll see how transparency turns crises into shared wins, how fit and purpose keep people engaged, and how bold moves pay off when paired with self-awareness.

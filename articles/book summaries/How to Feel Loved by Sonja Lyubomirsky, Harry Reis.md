#### Introduction

## What’s in it for me? Stop trying to be more lovable and start feeling more loved.

A partner who notices you’re off before you’ve said a word. A friend who checks in on exactly the right day. Someone who pushes back on your self-doubt because they see something in you that you don’t. These moments don’t just feel good. They feel like being truly known… or loved. Many people move through life with a nagging sense that the love they receive only reaches the surface. It often warms the polished version of themselves they’ve presented to the world instead of the fuller, messier, realer person underneath. Psychologists Sonja Lyubomirsky and Harry Reis have spent their careers studying happiness and close relationships, respectively. And their conclusion is unexpected: feeling loved has very little to do with being more lovable. All that effort to be funnier, more impressive, more attuned to what others want from you? It might get you admired. But to really feel loved, you need to stop performing. Maybe even more surprisingly, you need to make the other person feel loved first. In this Blink, you’ll discover a model for how genuine connection actually works and five mindsets that can transform the way you show up in your most important relationships.Let’s get started.

#### Chapter 1 of 6

## The relationship sea-saw (and why feeling loved starts with making others feel loved first)

Picture a seesaw, but underwater – hence the name sea-saw. You and another person sit on opposite ends. Each of you has a rich, complicated inner world, but most of it stays hidden below the surface. What’s visible above the waterline is the version of yourself you present: polished, composed, socially acceptable. The rest, including all the fears, insecurities, opinions you don’t broadcast, and parts you protect, stays submerged. But when you press down on your side by turning your full attention toward the other person, you lift them. More of who they actually are rises above the waterline. They feel seen. And almost automatically, they want to reciprocate. So they press down, and you rise. This back-and-forth is how real intimacy forms. Each moment of connection makes the next one easier. Brain-imaging research has found that during these types of exchanges, the neural patterns of speaker and listener start to sync. Feeling truly understood by someone is, in a very literal sense, a meeting of minds. Take the coming-of-age movie The Breakfast Club. Five teenagers with nothing in common spend a Saturday in detention. They start by performing their social roles, mocking each other and keeping their guards up. Then one person lets something real slip. Another responds with something real back. By the end, they’ve each taken turns lifting and being lifted, and they walk out feeling connected in a way none of them expected. That’s the sea-saw doing exactly what it’s supposed to do. One thing has to be true for any of this to work, though: both people have to be invested in the sea-saw. If you’re doing all the lifting – asking, giving, showing up – and getting nothing back, that’s exhausting. And it’s worth asking whether your counterpart deserves your energy. So what actually gets the sea-saw moving in the first place? That’s what we’ll spend the rest of this Blink exploring, starting with the first and maybe most underrated mindset of all: radical curiosity.

#### Chapter 2 of 6

## The radical curiosity mindset makes people feel worth knowing

Chances are, the last time someone made you feel interesting, they weren’t the most impressive person in the room. They were just the most curious about you. That’s the whole idea behind radical curiosity. And it turns out the difference between ordinary interest and the kind that makes people feel loved comes down to one unexpected ingredient: enthusiasm. Here’s a quick experiment that illustrates why. Pair up with someone and spend two minutes sharing your most meaningful experience from the past year, while they listen with zero expression – completely unmoved, no reaction, no encouragement. Then switch: they share something mundane, and you respond with over-the-top energy, leaning in, eyes wide, like “Wow, tell me more!” The unmoved listener leaves speakers doubting whether their experience was even worth sharing. The enthusiastic one makes a grocery store trip sound like an adventure. Psychologists call this capitalization – sharing something good with someone and having them amplify it back to you. Research shows that when a listener responds with authentic excitement, the speaker remembers the event more positively, feels closer to the listener, and reports higher well-being overall. Meanwhile, a lukewarm “that’s nice” followed by a subject change does the opposite. One study even found that how enthusiastically a partner responds to good news predicts whether a couple stays together better than how they handle conflict. That’s right, how you celebrate together matters more than how you fight! So what does radical curiosity look like day to day? Start by asking bigger questions. Move past the weather and the weekend – ask someone what drew them to their work, what the most useful mistake they’ve ever made was, or what they hope their life looks like a decade from now. People are far more open to personal questions than we tend to assume. When a topic does come up, follow it toward the person rather than the subject. That means if they mention their grandmother’s lasagna recipe, skip the ingredients and ask why it matters to them. The recipe is just the surface; the story underneath is where connection actually lives. And show up with energy. Lean in. Let your face reflect what you’re hearing. Ask follow-up questions that prove you were paying attention. There’s no formula, but you need to convey an unmistakable signal: there’s nowhere else I’d rather be than in this conversation with you. None of this demands a naturally extroverted personality. Research following people who practiced curiosity deliberately for just one week found they ended it happier, with a stronger sense of self-worth. Curiosity can be cultivated. When you show someone you find them genuinely fascinating, they open up. They start to see themselves as worth knowing. And almost inevitably, they become curious about you in return.

#### Chapter 3 of 6

## The sharing mindset allows others to love the real you

There’s a reason deathbed regrets so often involve things left unsaid. Keeping yourself hidden from the people you love feels safe, but it slowly makes feeling loved almost impossible. Think about it this way. If someone tells you that you’re brilliant, funny, or kind, but they’re only responding to the version of yourself you’ve carefully curated for public consumption, does it actually land? Or does some part of you think, But they don’t really know me? That gap between who you actually are and who others think you are is exactly where the feeling of being loved gets lost. This is why sharing matters. Not performing, not oversharing, not trauma-dumping, but letting people in on who you are beneath the polish. We’re talking strong opinions, fears, your embarrassing stories, the things you’re bad at, the parts of yourself you’re not entirely sure about. Research shows that people are far more interested in these things than we tend to think, and far more charitable in their responses than we fear. One study found that when people were asked to have a deep, personal conversation with a stranger and really open up, the conversations went better than expected every single time. We consistently overestimate how much others will judge us, and underestimate how much they actually want to connect. There’s also the question of secrets. Studies suggest the average person is carrying around 13 of them. And while most aren’t anywhere near as dramatic as assuming a false identity after committing a crime – yes, that actually happened – even ordinary secrets create distance. They take up mental energy, make interactions feel slightly performative, and make it hard to fully receive love when it’s offered, because you know the person offering it hasn’t seen the whole picture. Sharing doesn’t require a grand confession. Just start with something real but manageable. Mix the serious with the light. Give the other person room to respond and to share back. Notice whether they’re with you before going deeper. There are a few things worth avoiding when you’re sharing. First up is “boomerasking,” or asking someone a question as a thin excuse to talk about yourself. Oversharing too soon before trust has been established can also be an issue. And then there’s the opposite trap of hiding behind “boundaries” in a way that keeps everyone at arm’s length indefinitely. The goal isn’t vulnerability for its own sake. It’s letting someone see enough of the real you that, when they respond with warmth, you actually feel it.

#### Chapter 4 of 6

## The listening-to-learn mindset makes people feel heard

Most people think they’re pretty good listeners. But think is the key word there. In one study, a whopping 94 percent of people overestimated how well they listened in conversations with strangers. The problem isn’t effort, it’s approach. Most of us listen to respond. We’re already composing our reply, preparing our rebuttal, or waiting for our moment with the mic. That’s hearing, not listening. And it leaves the other person feeling vaguely unsatisfied, like they spoke into a void. Listening to learn is something different. It involves showing up to a conversation with one goal: to understand the other person’s experience as fully as possible. Not to fix it, not to match it with your own, just to get it. Research shows that when people feel truly listened to, they become less defensive, more open, and more willing to be vulnerable – which is exactly what the sea-saw needs to move. So what does high-quality listening actually look like? Three things matter most. First, attention. That means eye contact, body turned toward the speaker, phone out of reach. Small signals – a nod, a well-timed pause – that show you’re still with them without pulling them out of their story. Second, comprehension and elaboration. This is where most listeners stop short. Understanding what someone said isn’t enough – you have to show them you understood, and then invite them deeper. The best way is with what you might call depth questions. Not “How was your day?” or even “What happened next?” but the kind that goes underneath the story. “What did that feel like?” and “What made that moment stay with you?” These questions are hard to fake, which is exactly why they land so hard when asked with care. Third, positive intent. You can tick every box of attentive listening and still leave someone cold if your responses feel critical or detached. The goal is a nonjudgmental presence, where the other person feels safe enough to speak their truth. A few traps are worth knowing about – and avoiding. Stereotyping is when you assume you already know someone based on the group they belong to. Projection is when you assume they’d feel the way you’d feel in their situation. And transference is when a new person unconsciously reminds you of someone from your past, and you respond to that memory instead of the actual human in front of you. A listening-to-learn mindset disrupts all three of these pitfalls because when you’re actively curious about who someone actually is, your assumptions have less room to fill in the gaps. Unsurprisingly, the payoff of being a good listener runs in both directions. Studies show that individuals who identify as such feel more loved by partners, friends, family, and colleagues alike. Turns out the best way to feel heard is to become someone worth talking to.

#### Chapter 5 of 6

## The open heart mindset turns caring into connection

Imagine an AI chatbot that listens with total focus, asks all the right questions, and never gets distracted by its own agenda. Researchers actually built one, and people who used it felt genuinely heard. The bot nailed attentiveness. It nailed curiosity. But did anyone believe it actually wanted them to be happy? No. That’s the gap the open heart mindset fills. At its core, this mindset means caring about someone’s well-being – not just in the abstract, but enough to act on it. Research asking people what makes them feel loved found the top answers weren’t, in fact, impressive listening skills or clever questions. It was things like “cares for me” and “shows compassion in difficult times.” One of the most powerful expressions of this is what relationship scientists call the Michelangelo effect: seeing another’s potential before they see it themselves, and doing something about it. Like the sculptor who claimed to see the angel in the marble before he started carving, you can help bring out a version of someone they haven’t fully grown into yet. This doesn’t require grand gestures – it might mean creating space for a partner to pursue something they’ve shelved, or simply saying “I think you’re more capable of this than you realize” and meaning it. But the open heart mindset isn’t only for close relationships. It shows up just as powerfully in smaller moments. For instance, people who do kind acts for acquaintances and strangers report greater happiness than those who do kind acts for themselves. One study found that workers who showed generosity toward colleagues spread it too, with recipients paying it forward at nearly three times the rate of the control group. Most people still hold back more than they’d like to. They don’t share the compliment that comes to mind. They don’t reach out when they think of someone. We consistently underestimate how much our gestures will mean to others, and overestimate how awkward they’ll feel. We’re miscalibrated in exactly the direction that costs us connection. One last dimension of this mindset points inward. Treating yourself with the same compassion you’d extend to a friend going through something hard isn’t just good mental hygiene, it’s a prerequisite for fully receiving love from others. When you don’t feel worthy of love, other people’s warmth tends to bounce off. When you do, it lands.

#### Chapter 6 of 6

## The multiplicity mindset means loving someone fully, not just the easy parts

Did you know the way we typically describe people – he’s conscientious, she’s anxious, they’re selfish – is mostly wrong? Not because those labels are inaccurate, but because they’re incomplete. Studies tracking behavior across days found that variation within a single person fluctuates more from moment to moment than it differs between people. Most of us swing between generous and selfish, brave and cowardly, warm and withholding… sometimes within the same afternoon. People aren’t averages. They’re probability distributions. And loving someone well means holding that complexity without flinching. Think about Johnny Cash: brilliant songwriter, troubled addict, tender husband, frequent liar. June Carter didn’t resolve that contradiction. She held it. She saw both the man he was and the man he could become, and she chose to stay with the whole of him. And honestly? That might be one of the most profound things one person can do for another. The multiplicity mindset works in two directions. Toward others, it means resisting the pull toward one-dimensional judgments. When someone reveals something that makes you uncomfortable, the instinct is to flatten them into that moment. The multiplicity mindset asks you to stay curious instead – to look for the fuller picture, consider the history behind the behavior, and respond with the most generous interpretation available. Toward yourself, it means recognizing that no single failure, flaw, or regret gets to define you. Psychologists call this self-complexity, and it turns out to be a real buffer against hardship. When one part of your life is struggling, a multifaceted sense of self gives you somewhere else to stand. The biggest obstacle to both is shame, defined by researcher Brené Brown as the belief that a flaw makes you fundamentally unworthy of love. Shame thrives on secrecy. It pushes people to hide the parts of themselves they fear would repel others, and that makes real connection nearly impossible. The antidote isn’t pretending those parts don’t exist. It’s seeing them in context – as one thread in a much bigger, more complicated tapestry. Forgiveness is where this mindset gets its hardest test. Holding someone’s complexity absolutely doesn’t mean excusing harm. But it does mean being willing, when trust has been rebuilt, to release the desire for punishment and move forward. Studies show that people who practice forgiveness report less stress, more relationship satisfaction, and better overall health. Not because forgiveness is easy, but because carrying resentment indefinitely costs more. Connection rarely transforms all at once. It shifts one conversation at a time, one small act of courage at a time. Someone has to go first. It might as well be you.

#### Conclusion

## Final summary

In this Blink to How to Feel Loved by Sonja Lyubomirsky and Harry Reis, you’ve learned that feeling loved has less to do with being more lovable and more to do with how you show up for others. You’ve seen how the relationship sea-saw works – that real intimacy forms through a back-and-forth of lifting and being lifted, and that the person who goes first sets the whole thing in motion. You’ve discovered that radical curiosity makes people feel worth knowing. That sharing authentically gives others something real to respond to. That listening to learn means bringing attention, depth, and warmth to the words you’re hearing. That an open heart, directed at others and at yourself, is what turns understanding into love. And that the multiplicity mindset – holding someone’s full, contradictory, complicated humanity without flinching – is what makes love durable. None of these mindsets require you to be a different person. They just ask you to show up a little more openly, a little more curiously, and a little more bravely than you did yesterday.

#### Introduction

## What’s in it for me? Discover the breakthrough technologies shaping tomorrow and why they matter today.

Every generation inherits a world built by earlier breakthroughs. You flick a switch, charge your phone, board a plane, cross a bridge, stream a movie, or ask a machine a question – usually without giving a second thought to the science quietly holding it all together. But many of those same conveniences now sit at the center of our biggest problems. The way we produce energy, move around, build cities, manufacture goods, and process information has brought comfort and speed – but it’s also straining the very systems that keep life on Earth going.

So, does that make science the villain? Hardly. After all, science isn’t only a source of disruption – it’s also one of the best repair kits we’ve got. Across laboratories, construction sites, power grids, and data centers, researchers and engineers are rethinking the basics of modern life. Some ideas sound futuristic, like fusion energy, electric aircraft, wooden skyscrapers, quantum computers, and lifelike robots. Others are quieter, like better batteries, smarter recycling, cleaner concrete, and more resilient infrastructure.

In this Blink, you’ll explore how innovation is reshaping four foundations of daily life: energy, electricity, infrastructure, and information. You’ll see why the future depends less on one spectacular invention than on many technologies working together, each solving a different part of the same puzzle. Let’s start with the biggest question of all: how to power the world without overheating it.

#### Chapter 1 of 4

## Clean energy needs a whole system, not a single miracle

In California’s Livermore Valley, behind fences and armed security, scientists at the National Ignition Facility are trying to do something almost absurdly ambitious: recreate the process that powers the sun. Their target is tiny – roughly the size of a pencil eraser – yet the building around it is enormous. The reason is simple. If nuclear fusion can be controlled at useful scale, it could produce vast amounts of power with no carbon emissions, far less radioactive waste than today’s nuclear plants, and fuel sources that are either abundant or potentially self-sustaining.

That dream matters more than it might seem, because the world’s current energy system is still built around an older form of sunlight. Coal, oil, and natural gas began as ancient plant and ocean life that stored solar energy long ago. Even now, those fuels supply more than 80 percent of global energy – and they’re popular for good reason. They’re dense, relatively cheap, and already woven into transportation, heating, manufacturing, and electricity generation. But there’s a catch you already know. Burning them adds carbon dioxide and methane to the atmosphere, pushing climate change further along.

Here’s where a bit of physics helps. The clean-energy challenge begins with a basic rule. Energy cannot be created from nothing – it can only change form. A dam stores the motion of flowing water as height. A wind turbine turns moving air into electricity. A solar panel converts light into current.

Every technology is really an energy-conversion machine, and every machine comes with limits. Heat gets wasted, materials wear down, and geography decides what works best. This is exactly why the energy transition will look different from place to place. A windy, coastal nation such as Denmark can lean heavily on renewables, but even it has not eliminated fossil fuels. China illustrates a striking paradox: it remains the world’s largest consumer of dirty coal while simultaneously operating as the undisputed global leader and supplier of green energy. In the United States, a long-term shift from coal to natural gas historically lowered emissions, but a recent spike in overall electricity demand forced a temporary rebound in coal burning, proving how far the country remains from a truly carbon-free system.

Now, solar and wind are central to all this, but they can’t do it alone. Yes, solar panels have become cheaper and easier to deploy, from remote traffic lights to large fields of photovoltaic cells. Yet typical panels convert only a fraction of incoming sunlight into electricity, and they stop producing when the sun goes down. Morocco’s Noor complex points to one answer: use concentrated solar heat and molten salt to keep generating power after dark. Hoover Dam suggests another: pump water uphill when renewable power is plentiful, then let it flow down later to generate electricity when demand rises. Other tools plug the remaining gaps.

Biofuels can recycle carbon through plant growth, though they cannot replace everything. Geothermal power works especially well where Earth’s heat is close to the surface. Nuclear fission already produces carbon-free electricity, while smaller reactors may make nuclear power more flexible. Carbon capture, mineral storage, and geoengineering ideas may help too – though each carries its own cost, engineering, and political baggage.

Fusion remains the most dramatic prize, but it’s not ready to rescue the grid tomorrow. The real lesson is more practical and more hopeful: a cleaner future depends on combining many imperfect tools into one smarter energy system. Now, let’s follow that power out of the grid and into motion – into the cars, planes, and devices racing to run on electricity instead of fuel.

#### Chapter 2 of 4

## Electricity can power a cleaner future if the grid grows up with it

At sunrise in central Florida, a strange little aircraft called Hexa rises from the ground under 18 spinning propellers. It looks more like an oversized drone than a conventional aircraft, and that is exactly the point. Electric vertical takeoff vehicles can lift, hover, and land without a runway, while producing far less local pollution and noise than helicopters. Aviation contributes about 2.

5 percent of global CO₂, while cars and vans contribute about 10 percent, so moving transportation onto electricity is one of the clearest ways to cut pollution from daily life. Electricity is so useful because it is easy to move and easy to control. At its core, it comes from charged particles in motion. That may sound abstract, but the same basic relationship between electricity and magnetism runs through generators, motors, transformers, and the power lines outside your home. Spin a coil in a magnetic field and you can make current. Feed current into the right kind of motor and you can create motion.

That simple exchange is why electricity can be produced in one place and used miles away to cool food, run factories, charge phones, or move vehicles. It might surprise you that the idea of an electric vehicle is not actually new. In 1900, there were more electric cars than gasoline cars on American roads. Early models were quiet and clean, yet they were held back by weak batteries, slow charging, and short range. Then Henry Ford’s mass-produced Model T helped gasoline dominate the twentieth century. Now the balance is shifting again because lithium-ion batteries pack far more energy into a smaller, lighter package.

Lithium is especially useful because it’s the lightest metal, which matters when every extra pound reduces a car’s range or an aircraft’s flight time. Think of today’s electric vehicles as clever energy systems on wheels. Their batteries feed power through electronics that control the motor, and regenerative braking recovers some energy that would otherwise be lost as heat. The obstacles left are ones you probably already feel as a driver: you want longer range, quicker charging, and more confidence that chargers will actually be there when needed. But the bigger test sits behind the plug. A society full of electric cars, aircraft, heat pumps, data centers, and appliances will need a grid that can handle demand minute by minute.

Pumped storage offers one proven approach. At Virginia’s Bath County facility, water can rush downhill through turbines when demand rises, then be pumped back up when demand falls. Smart grids add another layer, using real-time data so power suppliers can smooth peaks without most households noticing. The grid also has to survive a rougher world.

Heat, wildfires, floods, and geomagnetic disturbances can damage equipment that takes months to replace. Long-distance transmission lines can move wind or solar power from where it is abundant to where people need it, but building them means permits, land rights, and public resistance. Electricity already runs modern life. In the next section, we’ll look at the materials and structures needed to build that future in the physical world.

#### Chapter 3 of 4

## The future will be built from smarter materials

In Milwaukee, a 284-foot apartment tower stands on a wood-based structural frame. That may sound like a step backward, until you look closely at what kind of wood it is. The material is mass timber, made by bonding smaller pieces into beams, panels, and floors strong enough for tall buildings. Ascent, that timber-concrete hybrid tower, captures the whole idea behind the next wave of infrastructure: the future depends on rebuilding the physical world with materials that are cleaner, stronger, easier to reuse, and better matched to the planet’s limits.

Here’s the problem, though – construction has a huge environmental footprint. We lean on concrete and steel because they’re strong, reliable, and familiar – but both come with a steep bill. Concrete depends on sand, which is being stripped from shorelines and riverbeds. Steel takes enormous energy to make, and producing one metric ton can release nearly two metric tons of carbon dioxide. Since cities keep expanding upward and outward, cleaner construction is one of the biggest engineering problems of modern life. So, where do we start?

Mass timber offers a surprisingly practical answer. It’s lighter than concrete and steel, which can reduce the need for massive foundations. Prefabricated wooden components can speed construction, and wood naturally helps insulate buildings, lowering heating and cooling demands. If you’re worried about fire, its performance is also better than you might assume. Large timber beams char on the outside, creating a protective layer that helps preserve the interior structure. Ascent’s materials were put through intense heat testing – and as a bonus, all that wood locks away carbon that would otherwise drift into the atmosphere.

Still, timber cannot replace every material. A cleaner built environment also means improving the materials we already use. Researchers are working on lower-carbon concrete, concrete that can repair its own cracks, longer-lasting steel alloys, and glass that can adjust to sunlight. The goal isn’t to abandon cities, roads, bridges, and high-rises. The goal is to make them with fewer emissions, less waste, and longer useful lives. The material problem reaches far beyond buildings.

Electric vehicles, wind turbines, solar panels, batteries, phones, and computers all depend on lithium, cobalt, nickel, and rare earth elements. These materials make clean technology possible – yet pulling them from the ground can scar landscapes, drain water, and lock nations into uneasy dependence. Take the Clarion-Clipperton Zone in the Pacific, which lays the dilemma bare. Its seabed nodules contain valuable minerals, yet the same region hosts thousands of species, many of them barely known. And that’s why recycling becomes more than sorting bottles. Atoms don’t vanish when we throw things away.

Metals can often be recovered effectively, while plastics and lithium-ion batteries are much harder to reuse at scale. So better recycling will have to be baked into industrial design from the very first sketch. Then comes 3D printing, which turns construction into a software-guided process. Concrete frames for schools, printed housing developments, medical implants, and replacement parts all point in the same direction: use only what is needed, shape it precisely, and reduce waste. So, what makes that kind of precision possible? In the final section, we’ll look at the digital systems behind it.

#### Chapter 4 of 4

## Digital technology turns the world into usable information

There’s a humanoid robot named Sophia who can hold a conversation, answer questions, make facial expressions, and even appear to crack a joke. It feels almost eerie, doesn’t it? But the machinery behind her begins with a surprisingly plain idea. Everything Sophia does traces back to one basic move that powers all digital technology: breaking reality into information a computer can process.

A face, a song, a sentence, a bank transfer, a satellite image – all of these can be translated into patterns of 1s and 0s. Why does that matter so much? Because digital information can be copied, stored, searched, and transmitted with extraordinary precision. Take sound, for example. It begins as changing pressure traveling through the air. To digitize it, a computer breaks that smooth wave into tiny time slices, measures each slice, and stores the result as numbers.

A standard CD does this 44,100 times per second, which is enough detail for most ears to hear it as continuous sound. The same logic applies to images, text, and video: once information becomes bits, computers can manipulate it at high speed. The tiny workhorse behind this world is the transistor. Early computers used vacuum tubes, which were large, fragile, and constantly burning out. Transistors made switching smaller and more reliable, and modern chips now pack billions of switches into spaces small enough to fit inside everyday devices. That’s why a phone in your pocket can outperform room-sized machines from the early computer age.

That’s where the internet comes in – it took those isolated machines and turned them into a global information system. One early step was ARPANET, a U. S. research network that connected computers at universities and government-funded labs so they could exchange information across long distances. Its big breakthrough was packet switching, which solved a basic weakness of older communications. Instead of requiring one fixed route, a message can be broken into pieces, sent through available pathways, and reassembled at the end.

The World Wide Web later made that system useful to ordinary users by turning documents, images, and media into accessible pages. The internet is the underlying network; the web is the information moving across it. That network keeps stretching further. Cloud computing lets your files live in data centers rather than only on your own device. Convenient, yes, but it comes with a catch: dependency. If a server is hacked, damaged, or mismanaged, the user may have little control.

Satellite internet adds another layer. Starlink has placed more than 7,000 satellites in orbit as of May 2025, expanding internet access in places where cables and towers fall short. So, what’s the next leap? It may come from the internet of things, the idea of putting sensors and internet connections into ordinary objects so they can collect data and talk to each other. Think of a thermostat that learns your schedule, a farm sensor that reports soil moisture, or a factory machine that flags its own wear before it breaks. These connected devices are spreading into homes, farms, hospitals, factories, and vehicles.

By 2030, as many as 30 billion of them may be linked to the internet, pumping out useful streams of real-time data while opening fresh security risks along the way. Artificial intelligence sits on top of all this. Neural networks learn patterns from huge datasets, which lets systems generate text, recognize faces, guide robots, and make predictions. Yet they don’t think like humans, and they can produce convincing errors or reflect biases in their training data. So the real future of information comes down to one thing: understanding these systems well enough to use them wisely. The more clearly you see the machinery beneath the magic, the better prepared you are to shape what comes next.

#### Conclusion

## Final summary

The main takeaway of this Blink to *The Next Big Thing* by Rob Marciano and James Trefil is that innovation isn’t about waiting for one spectacular invention to solve everything. A better future will come from many advances working together: cleaner energy, smarter electrical systems, more sustainable materials, and digital tools that help us understand and manage complexity. Fusion power, electric aircraft, timber towers, 3D printing, quantum computers, and AI all point to the same larger shift. The challenge is to use science practically and responsibly, so the systems that power, move, build, and connect our world become cleaner, stronger, and more resilient.

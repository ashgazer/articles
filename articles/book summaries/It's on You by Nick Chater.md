#### Introduction

## What’s in it for me? Gain new insights on how we can change the world we live in.

When something goes wrong in society, it’s quite natural for people to start pointing fingers. It’s also human nature for us to gravitate toward the easier answer when it comes to casting blame. That generally means holding *individual behavior* accountable. Obesity epidemic?

Well, people just need to eat less. Climate crisis? Get everyone to drive less. The guiding narrative, over time, has been to see complex social problems as the result of individual decisions, and maybe we’ve even convinced ourselves that they can be solved, one person at a time. But there is another way to look at things. Instead of focusing only on individual choices, you can step back and examine the systems shaping those choices.

The fact is, we’re all influenced by rules, incentives, and environments that make certain outcomes more likely than others. In this Blink, we’ll look at how industries and institutions have steered our attention away from the real systemic changes that are needed, and how they’ve even used behavioral science to maintain the status quo. But we’ll also end on a hopeful note – one that points the way toward better outcomes and the ways in which we can redesign the systems themselves.

#### Chapter 1 of 6

## Reframing responsibility

These days, there are quite a few examples of crisis-level issues that are affecting societies and yet tend to be looked at as problems that could be solved if only people were to make better decisions. One of the best examples of such a crisis-level issue is climate change. There was a time when public awareness was growing at such a pace that it would be impossible for major fossil fuel companies to escape the blame. But instead of changing their plans, they did an expert job of reframing the conversation.

A big tool in their PR campaigns was the idea of *the personal carbon footprint*. Oil and gas companies like BP started putting calculators online, encouraging people to do the work of changing by measuring and reducing their own emissions. On the surface, it felt empowering. But what it’s really about is redirecting attention. Once the focus lands on personal habits – how often you fly, what you eat, how you heat your home – it takes your attention away from the larger system that is the main source of harmful emissions. There’s even a psychological aspect to this.

When people take small individual actions, like measuring their own carbon footprint, it can make them feel like, “Well, I’ve done my part. ” That, in turn, results in reduced support for broader policy change. It’s a phenomenon known as “crowding out,” when well-intentioned efforts can end up dampening momentum for the kinds of big-picture changes that would matter most. As a result, many of the most effective solutions, like carbon pricing or large-scale regulation, stay relegated to the back burner. Even though research shows that these are the solutions that can make the most difference, they would require major changes to the industry, which is something the industry will fight against – tooth and nail.

#### Chapter 2 of 6

## When systems shift, and the blame follows

While the climate crisis is a prime example, it’s far from the only one. So in the next couple sections we’re going to quickly look at a few more impactful areas of life – including obesity, retirement, and health care – and how they’ve been reshaped, at the system level, into problems that individuals are expected to solve on their own. In the US, obesity is an epidemic, affecting around 40 percent of the adult population – and that’s just adults. As with climate change, the dominant story once again puts the onus on personal discipline.

It states that people simply need to eat less and move more. But the data tells a different story. Fifty years ago, the obesity rate in the US wasn’t a whole lot different from other countries in the world. That all changed in the 1980s when there were big changes in the food environment which included cheaper processed foods, larger portion sizes, more aggressive marketing, and greater income disparity. One study even followed military families as they got assigned to new locations around the world. When those families moved to areas with higher obesity rates, their likelihood of becoming overweight correspondingly increased.

When they moved somewhere healthier, the risk dropped. In other words, it’s the environment – not just willpower – that shapes outcomes. Pricing is one way the food industry has been able to change people’s habits, for the worse. Fast food menus often make larger portions look like better value; when you only have to pay a small fraction more for a significantly larger portion of soda or fries – that’s a tough temptation to resist. These kinds of techniques, like fast food menu pricing, are called *nudging*. We’ll get into this in some more, but it’s basically a way of framing things that takes advantage of human nature in order to have people make a desired choice without thinking twice about it.

It can lead you to doubling your calorie intake at McDonald’s just as easily as it can shift your focus away from the areas where meaningful change actually needs to happen. The concept of nudging really took off in 2008, when the book *Nudge*, written by legal scholar Cass R. Sunstein and behavioral economist Richard Thaler, was published. It detailed the ways in which you could take human nature into consideration when presenting people with choices.

Do it the right way and you could increase the chances that people will make the desired, or “right,” choice rather than the wrong choice. When it’s done well, folks won’t even feel as though they’re having their autonomy taken away. As we’ll see in the sections ahead, nudging has gone on to be used in a number of different ways. Sometimes it’s trying to help individuals, but often it’s been a pervasive way to shift responsibility away from those who are creating the problems.

#### Chapter 3 of 6

## When the problem is everywhere

For much of the twentieth century, workers relied on what we call *defined-benefit pensions*, which guaranteed a stable income in old age. These plans pooled risk and placed responsibility on employers. It worked pretty well for a while, but over time, that system has been dismantled. Companies downsized and moved toward *defined-contribution plans*.

This was a major change as it shifted the burden of saving, investing, and managing risk away from them and onto individuals. Behavioral economics and nudging stepped in to help steer people with practices like making automatic enrollment into certain plans a default choice. While these policies can help, on average, people are losing around 40 percent of their savings prior to retirement. Most, of course, don’t realize this; nudging is being used to influence employees who are being asked to make complex decisions that are beyond their comprehension. Health care reveals a similar story, but one with even higher stakes. The US spends far more than any comparable country – around $13,000 per person annually – yet rates worse on many measures, including maternal mortality and life expectancy.

Again, the responsibility is largely placed on the patient, who is expected to navigate an incredibly complex system with a variety of different plans. Meanwhile, the system is designed with faulty payment structures that reward volume over value. This means that doctors and hospitals are paid for every procedure, test, or treatment they provide, which incentivizes them to do more, even when doing more isn’t necessarily helpful. This leads us to the opioid crisis in the US. Here, Purdue Pharma funded medical organizations and academic work to promote opioid prescribing. The American Medical Association, after receiving a $3 million gift from Purdue, even developed a training course to encourage doctors to be less cautious with opioid prescriptions.

But when addiction spread, the narrative suddenly shifted toward individual blame rather than the system that enabled the problem. These issues of systemic problems being foisted upon individuals extend even further, to issues of pollution and plastic waste, online privacy, and even road safety. They all follow the same script: systems are designed in profitable ways that nonetheless harm society, but the people behind those systems shift the narrative to make those outcomes feel like the result of individual choices. Inequality is one of the clearest examples. Tax systems have been changed over the years to continually protect the wealth of the top percenters and limit social mobility. In the US, 43 percent of children born into the bottom fifth of the income distribution stay there as adults, while only 4 percent reach the top.

Meanwhile, 40 percent of children born into the top fifth remain there. Yet despite these patterns, the US often touts itself as being a meritocracy, where talent and effort are the deciding factors in determining where you land in society. In other words, it’s on you. And even people who are disadvantaged by the uneven playing field continue to buy into this claim, which only helps to sustain the system.

#### Chapter 4 of 6

## The myth that shapes everything

Okay, now that we’ve looked at a number of the issues, let’s zoom out and look at things from a more macro perspective, to better understand how we got here. Ever since the US recovered from the Great Depression, a strong narrative has taken hold about how the world is supposed to work. The authors refer to it as *the Big Myth*. To put it very succinctly, influential economists convinced the nation’s leadership that markets are inherently efficient, individuals are responsible, and government is the problem.

That myth really took root in the 1980s under Ronald Reagan’s tenure. He went all-in on pushing the idea that small government was the answer, with fewer regulations to get in the way of a flourishing economy. Taxes on top earners were cut from 70 percent to 28 percent, unions were weakened, and regulations were rolled back. If corporations and executives got rich, the rest would sort itself out. The Big Myth was so influential that it reshaped how people interpreted almost everything – from inequality to health care to climate policy. Even Britain got onboard under Margaret Thatcher, and Democrats in the US, like Bill Clinton and Barack Obama did little to reverse the narrative.

Granted, it isn’t easy to turn it around because influential groups like the National Association of Manufacturers have spent decades funding campaigns to further promote an anti-regulatory worldview. But the reality is that the Big Myth is flawed. It rests on a simplified, outdated worldview that assumes individuals are rational decision-makers with full access to all the pertinent information. And it tends to overlook things like unequal bargaining power and external threats like pollution. It also leans heavily on what the economist Albert Hirschman called “perversity arguments” – the idea that even well-intentioned policies and regulations will naturally backfire. One famous example claimed that seat belt laws would backfire by leading to riskier driving.

Of course, that claim proved to be false. Even though it always requires huge uphill battles against the car industry, safety regulations like seat belts and air bags have saved countless lives. Nevertheless, confidence in the government has weakened and, by and large, the new era of behavioral economics hasn't helped. Rather than helping people make better decisions, it led to the public policy term of “ *responsibilization* ”.

It’s up to us to pick healthier foods, manage our privacy, or learn the abyrinthine world of health insurance and pension plans – even when these systems are designed to be confusing or exploitative. All of this results in an imbalance that distracts us from improving things and changing the systems that are truly responsible for so much of our quality of life. But in the final sections we’ll dig into why we shouldn’t give up that fight just yet.

#### Chapter 5 of 6

## Fixing the system

What would it actually look like to design systems that work with human behavior instead of against it? While we’ve pointed out a lot of its failings so far, behavioral economics could be used for good – especially given new technologies. Speed limitations could be placed in cars to prevent reckless driving, much in the way ignition locks currently stop drunk drivers. These approaches don’t try to “fix” behavior – they acknowledge the issue and change the system itself.

Currently, we’re surrounded by industries designing systems that exploit predictable human tendencies. Health insurers, for example, often lure people in with attractive plans and then raise prices later, relying on inertia to keep customers from switching. Two things that can help move a society in the right direction are *factual information* and *transparency* on where money is going. Taxes are always a tough subject, but when people understand how taxes work – like the difference between a tax credit (which tends to benefit lower-income families) and a tax deduction (which tends to benefit the wealthy) – they’ll be in a better position to see that, sometimes, taxes are a solution. As it is, any kind of “tax” is often framed as a negative, even though introducing a carbon tax could be one of the most effective ways to reduce emissions. If the government could clearly explain how the revenues from a carbon tax could be used to boost Social Security or fund other popular programs, maybe we could change people’s attitudes toward an acceptable change to the current system.

One example we can always look to is the change to smoking regulations. It was a long and hard-fought battle against a massive campaign of resistance by the tobacco industry, but the US made great strides in reducing the smoking rate and changing the popular perception about the dangers of cigarettes. New taxes and advertising regulations eventually reshaped the environment. The same could happen in any of the areas we’ve been talking about, be it health care, pollution, or the food or energy industries. But one other thing is needed: a healthy democracy. The political system, like any other system, can be shaped – and sometimes distorted – by concentrated power.

And some of the most consequential changes happen under the radar. Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act is one example. It’s a relatively obscure provision that ended up shielding major tech platforms from being held liable for the content it publishes, thereby shaping the sometimes indecent structure of the modern internet. Media takeovers, funding for think tanks, selective research, and the spread of misinformation can all tilt how issues are understood.

And when trust in our institutions erodes, it becomes harder to agree on basic facts, let alone solutions. That’s why strengthening the political process becomes so important. Reducing the influence of money, increasing transparency, and supporting independent media sources won’t immediately solve our problems. But it will lay the foundation for a system that has a better chance of being fair and balanced.

#### Chapter 6 of 6

## Why change is so difficult, and why there’s always hope

To put it simply, people are naturally resistant to change. Even when that change would clearly make things better, it can be a hard sell. And the reason has a lot to do with how our minds work. Humans are actually great at adapting.

But this is a double-edged sword. On one side we can quickly adjust to whatever world we’re living in, but on the other side, it means we grow accustomed to systems that are inefficient, unfair, or even harmful. For us, it becomes the new normal and any disruption to it can feel risky or uncomfortable, even if the new system would be a huge improvement. But that’s just a hurdle we have to clear, and the good news is that we’ve done exactly that many times in the past. Again, look at smoking bans. Before we started getting cigarettes out of bars, restaurants and work places, many were crying about lost freedoms and how badly businesses would suffer.

But now, smoke-free environments are the new normal and many of today’s smokers would find it unpleasant to go back to how it was. More recently, congestion pricing has followed a nearly identical path. In Stockholm, public opposition was strong before the policy took effect. But after people experienced less traffic and smoother commutes, support rose sharply. The same pattern played out in cities like London, Oslo, and Milan. What began as an unpopular charge gradually became accepted – and even valued – once its benefits were felt.

The same can be said about taxing sugary drinks and banning trans fats. But once we get past the noise and start to enact the changes, we realize that we might just be better off with rules that protect us from ourselves. Another factor to consider is *time*. When change happens slowly, it can go undetected. Take wealth inequality, for example. The wage gap has been growing steadily in the US since the 1980s, to the point where it can seem normal to some.

Imagine if this gap had happened suddenly. There’d surely be protests in the street demanding change. A slow, steady increase over decades is easier to absorb. Which is why the incremental threats of climate change and democratic erosion can slip under the radar. But that doesn’t make them any less important. It’s up to us to keep the resistance alive, especially when so many benefit from maintaining the status quo.

Look to the civil rights movement, labor protections, and the public health measures we’ve succeeded with in the past. These all required sustained pressure and collective action. Change doesn’t have to feel perfect at the outset; it just has to get started. Once it does, people adjust – and the new system starts to feel like the way things should have been all along.

#### Conclusion

## Final summary

The main takeaway of this Blink to *It’s on You* by Nick Chater and George Loewenstein is that many of society’s biggest problems persist because we keep looking in the wrong place. We’re encouraged to focus on individual behavior – our choices, habits, and responsibility – while the real drivers sit in the systems around us. Whether it’s climate change, inequality, health care, or addiction, powerful interests shape the rules of the game and then promote a story that those outcomes are the result of personal decisions. But real progress comes from redesigning those systems – changing incentives, strengthening regulation, and making democratic processes more resilient to influence.

Behavioral science still has a role, but it’s most powerful when it helps design better rules, protect people from exploitation, and build public support for reform. Change often feels uncomfortable at first, yet people adapt quickly once new policies are in place, as seen with smoking bans, congestion pricing, and sugar taxes. That pattern matters, because it suggests many worthwhile reforms are far more acceptable in practice than they appear in theory. History shows that broader change can happen with persistence. Only then will we reshape the rules so they serve the many rather than the few. Okay, that’s it for this Blink.

#### Introduction

## What’s in it for me? Learn how to stop fighting time and live well.

When Chris Guillebeau shared a blog post describing a feeling he’d struggled with for years – a low-grade, unnamed dread of time slipping away – hundreds of readers wrote back saying they recognized it instantly. For some, it was the biggest problem in their lives. The condition has a name: *time anxiety*. It’s worth distinguishing from FOMO – the fear of missing out – which stays rooted in the present moment, the sense that something is happening elsewhere and you’re being left out. Time anxiety reaches much further – touching the decisions you wish you’d made differently, the confusion of not knowing what to do right now, and a future that feels uncertain or foreclosed. It tends to show up in one of two ways. Some people feel it as a big-picture unease – a sense that life is passing without any clear direction or purpose. Others feel it more immediately, as the daily pressure of too much to do and too little time. Most people wrestle with both. When that feeling strikes, your instinct is probably to get more organized – a better system, a restructured schedule. The trouble is, that tends to deepen the problem rather than resolve it. That’s where this Blink comes in. You’ll discover why better planning was never going to be enough, learn to recognize the mental patterns keeping you stuck, and find a more sustainable – and less anxious – way of moving through your days. Ready? Let’s get started.

#### Chapter 1 of 6

## Free yourself from the myth of time management

Time. Think about your relationship with it for a moment. It moves through your most productive days and your worst ones with complete indifference – it simply doesn’t care. Yet a whole industry – books, seminars, apps, keynote speakers charging tens of thousands of dollars – has been built on the premise that you can manage it. That premise has always been shaky, to say the least. After all, you can no more manage time than you can manage the weather. So why does this matter? Because believing the myth costs you. When you accept that time management is exactly that – a myth – you stop spending energy on a battle you were never going to win. The mindset that actually helps is called *radical acceptance*, and the idea behind it is simple: some degree of pain is built into life. What turns that pain into suffering is the energy spent resisting it. Applied to time, this means accepting that your days will never be fully under your control – and discovering that once you stop fighting that fact, something genuinely useful happens. The mental energy that was going into the battle becomes available for things you can actually influence. From there, a few practical steps can help relieve the immediate pressure. Start with *time decluttering*. Go through your calendar and ask yourself honestly: which upcoming commitments still deserve to be there? Clearing even two or three opens up real breathing room. Next, cut down the number of channels people can use to reach you. Email, workplace messaging, social media – every one of them is a claim on your attention. Stepping back from even one gives you more control over where your focus goes. The third move is more internal. Many people with time anxiety carry a fixed story about themselves – that this is just how they are, and always will be. Replacing that with something more open – for example, “I’m still working this out,” or “I’m making progress” – matters more than it might seem. The way you talk to yourself about time shapes how you engage with it. Understanding why you think about time the way you do is the next piece of the puzzle – and that’s where things get interesting.

#### Chapter 2 of 6

## Stop believing your brain when it lies to you about time

If time anxiety had an engine, it would be the way our minds distort reality when we’re under pressure. These distortions – psychologists call them *cognitive distortions* – are patterns of irrational thinking that feel completely real in the moment. Catastrophizing does the most damage: a single missed deadline becomes proof of permanent failure; forgetting a name means you must have dementia. Your thoughts spiral, and before you know it, a small setback feels like it defines everything. So, how do you fight back? Start with a technique called *thought countering*. When a negative thought takes hold, the first step is simply to name what’s happening – to identify it as a distortion rather than a fact. Then challenge it: is this based on reality or assumption? Have you faced something similar before and come through it? Finally, replace the distorted thought with something more grounded. “I’m going to fail this project” might become “missing one deadline doesn’t mean the whole thing falls apart – what’s the next step?” Now, it turns out there’s often a second culprit feeding your time anxiety, and this one runs deeper: *time blindness*. It’s a condition in which the brain genuinely struggles to track the passing of time accurately. What seemed like plenty of time evaporates without warning, and suddenly you’re in panic mode. Or you put something off for days dreading it, only to find it takes ten minutes. Time blindness isn’t carelessness, though – it’s a genuine difference in how some people process time, and it’s particularly common in people with ADHD. So, what actually helps? Stop trying to track time mentally and make it visible instead. Make sure clocks are positioned where you’ll actually see them throughout your home. A visual timer on your desk helps too – these come in a range of colors and are far more intuitive than relying on memory. Set alarms well ahead of when you need to be somewhere, rather than trusting your own sense of how long you have. And wherever possible, give your full attention to one task before moving to the next. Attempting several things at once without finishing any of them is one of the most reliable routes to feeling like time is constantly escaping you. Once you can see those patterns clearly, a bigger question surfaces: what do you do about the work itself? The answer might surprise you.

#### Chapter 3 of 6

## You don’t need to do everything well

Many people work in jobs where there’s no finish line. One task completed simply reveals another waiting behind it, and without any clear sense of when something is finished, you can find yourself adding to work that was actually done an hour ago. The result is that familiar feeling of never quite catching up – even on days when you’ve been working hard the whole time. It turns out the fix is deceptively simple: decide in advance what “enough” looks like. Before starting any piece of work, ask yourself what *done* really means for this particular task. Not a vague sense of completion, but a specific finish line. Once you reach it, stop. You can always choose to continue, but having that marker changes the psychological experience of working entirely. Instead of measuring yourself against everything that remains undone, you have something concrete to feel satisfied about. Now, for the part that sounds almost wrong: not everything needs to be done well. For a great many tasks, doing them poorly is actually the right approach. If you’ve been putting off answering a message because you can’t find the perfect response, send an imperfect one. If your living space needs tidying but you’re exhausted, spend ten minutes on the parts that really matter and leave the rest. Quality still counts, yes – but here’s the thing that perfectionism hides from you: it burns a wild amount of energy on things that just don’t deserve it. We tend to wear perfectionism like a badge of honor. It really isn’t one, though. Instead, it undermines three things: momentum – because you can’t finish and move on; satisfaction – because no accomplishment ever feels real; and judgment – because you can’t tell the high-stakes tasks from the trivial ones. Give yourself permission to do some things poorly, and you free up real energy for the things that actually matter. It’s a conscious decision about where your best effort is most needed – and making that decision deliberately is one of the most effective ways to reduce the pressure you feel around time. So you know what deserves your best. Great. Actually sitting down to do it is something else, though – and that gap is where most of us quietly lose enormous amounts of time. Why do we dodge the very things we know we should do – and how can you break this pattern?

#### Chapter 4 of 6

## The things you dread are running your life

Think about the last time you spotted an unread email and felt your stomach tighten. Avoidance is a deeply instinctive response – when something feels threatening, your brain fires up the same escape reflex our ancestors used to dodge predators. The trouble is that the "threat" staring you down today isn’t a physical one. It’s that email sitting in your inbox, a conversation you keep pushing to next week, a task that’s been glaring at you from the top of the to-do list for two weeks. Sure, avoidance hands you immediate relief, but it comes with a cost that keeps accumulating. After all, whatever you’re putting off doesn’t disappear – it just sits in the background, consuming your energy even on the days when you’re not consciously thinking about it. So, how do you break the spell? A useful first step is to make what the author calls a *to-dread list* – a separate list consisting entirely of the things you’ve been avoiding. Once you have it, set aside around 30 minutes and work through only those items. Going in, it’ll feel uncomfortable. Coming out, you’ll feel lighter – and that shift is the whole point. Here's where it gets even better. The real win is stopping items from landing on that list in the first place, and one of the most effective ways to do that is to set up what you might call *rules of engagement*. These are default decisions made in advance about how you’ll handle recurring demands on your time. If you know that your most productive hours are in the morning, make a rule that meetings only happen after lunch. If family time in the evening matters to you, make that a standing commitment that other requests don’t override. The value of deciding these things in advance is that when a conflict arises, you don’t have to weigh it up from scratch every time. The decision is already made. A related pattern is what happens when small unmade decisions pile up. Every choice left hanging – what to have for lunch, whether to reply to a message, which of two options to take – adds to an accumulated drag that grinds you down over the course of a day. The practical response is to make decisions quickly and move on, handling things once rather than returning to them repeatedly. Each small decision made and moved past builds momentum, while each one deferred adds to the pile. And the same logic applies to things you’ve already started. Giving yourself permission to put down a book 50 pages in, leave a party when you stop enjoying it, or stop watching a series that’s run its course saves real time and builds genuine self-trust. For every hour spent on something that no longer deserves it, there’s something else available instead.

#### Chapter 5 of 6

## Learn to follow the rhythm of your energy

You know this pattern, even if you’ve never had a name for it. You get excited about something – a project, an idea, a goal – and throw yourself into it completely. For a while, everything flows. Then, almost without warning, you crash. Motivation drains away, simple tasks feel impossibly heavy, and the very thing you were so energized by now feels like a burden. This is what’s known as the *hyperfocus-burnout cycle*. Hyperfocus – the state of deep, absorbed attention where time seems to disappear – can be genuinely productive and even enjoyable. The mistake is assuming you can sustain it indefinitely. Your biology simply doesn’t allow it. And the harder you push past the point of exhaustion, the longer the recovery takes. So, how do you manage it? A practical way is to think of your energy in terms of a traffic light. On green days you’re focused and working well – so make the most of them. On yellow days you’re running below capacity. The right response is to lower your output expectations, switch to less demanding tasks, or work on something different entirely. On red days, pushing through is counterproductive. Rest is the only thing that actually works, and taking it deliberately is considerably better than having your body force it on you. Now, plenty of people feel guilty about resting, especially on yellow and red days. Looking at what you achieve across a full week rather than judging yourself day by day helps here. You’re more productive across a longer stretch than any single difficult day suggests. All of these tools point toward a bigger question – not just how to spend your time better, but why it matters so much in the first place. The answer, it turns out, starts with something most of us spend our lives trying not to think about.

#### Chapter 6 of 6

## Change how you live by thinking about death

While we all know we’re going to die, most of us rarely sit with this fact long enough to let it change anything. Not as an abstract fact about human beings in general, but personally, specifically. At some point our time runs out. Thinking about this every day sounds morbid. In practice it turns out to be one of the most clarifying things you can do. When you hold the fact of your own mortality close, a lot of what felt urgent stops feeling that way. Petty obligations, false deadlines, the pressure to respond to everything immediately – much of it falls away. What remains is a much cleaner sense of what actually deserves your attention. One way to bring this into focus is to think of your life as a movie you’re directing. You’ve already shot a good portion of it. What happens next is still yours to shape. A practical tool that helps with those choices is what the author calls the *closer or further filter*. When you’re facing a decision about how to spend your time, ask yourself one simple question: *Does this bring me closer to what matters, or further away?* The answer, when you’re honest with yourself, tends to come quickly. So, where does that leave you? Instead of trying to build a legacy – an outcome largely outside your control – focus on living well. This means caring for yourself and others, pursuing what genuinely matters, and releasing what doesn’t. It’s an achievable goal. And it starts with the time you have right now.

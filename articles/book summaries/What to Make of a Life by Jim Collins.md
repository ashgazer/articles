#### Introduction

## What’s in it for me? Uncover the truth about living meaningfully.

Have you ever wondered how people like Benjamin Franklin, Toni Morrison or Meryl Streep came to lead remarkable lives? How did they cultivate such deep passion and broad expertise in their respective fields? How did they overcome their inner battles and external struggles? In order to answer these questions, the author and his research team spent a decade studying the lives of influential people, from politicians and musicians to geneticists.

34 life stories were selected for detailed analysis – totaling nearly 2,800 years of human experience. The common denominators that emerged from the research uproot many beliefs we hold about success, discipline, and dealing with adversity. Using vivid examples, this Blink distills these revelatory findings into an inspirational guide for better self-knowledge. Rather than giving instructions, it will inspire you to chart your own path, navigate hardships with compassion, and sustain your passion well into later life.

#### Chapter 1 of 6

## Meaning is discovered, not engineered

How do you build a meaningful life? This question has puzzled generations of philosophers, creatives, entrepreneurs. Hopes of finding an answer to it might have led you to pick out this Blink. But have you ever considered that it might be the wrong question to ask?

Examining the lives of some of the most influential minds of our times, the author found that the path to a meaningful life is more often a process of discovery than the result of deliberate ambition. Take celebrated author Toni Morrison. Did you know that she had no plans of becoming a writer? She started writing on the side after putting her children to sleep, with the simple goal of creating something she herself would enjoy reading. Yet she quickly developed an all-consuming passion for the craft that led her to become one of the most influential Afroamerican authors of all time. This effortless obsession with an endeavor shows up time and time again in the lives of brilliant people.

They are consumed by their work – and happy about it. Pulitzer-winning historian Barbara Tuchmann started her career in her mid-30s, after having children and without any formal encouragement. Yet she wrote and rewrote so obsessively that her book on World War I came to influence John F. Kennedy during the Cuban Missile Crisis, possibly averting nuclear war. Barbara McClintock, who revolutionized genetics with her discovery of mobile genetic elements, once became so absorbed by a college exam on the topic she finished 20 minutes early – then sat the rest of the time in silence, unable to recall her own name. None of these remarkable women reported being self-disciplined about their work.

Deep engagement with their work wasn’t a chore but a compulsion: once they got going, they were genuinely unable to stop. They also weren’t in it for the money or the fame. Quite the opposite. After winning the Nobel Prize at age 81, McClintock reported being happy that the scientific community had ignored her for decades. It meant she could do her work in peace. For remarkable people, the love of doing proceeds, and exceeds, any outcome associated with it.

The intensity they bring to their work doesn’t drain them. It nourishes them. But how did they get there?

#### Chapter 2 of 6

## Follow your encodings, find your hedgehog

Remarkable people appear driven by an inner fire – a deep passion for their work that drives them forward. But what fuels it? The author found one principle that applied to every single person he researched. They all eventually found endeavors that simply clicked with them, like McClintock becoming consumed by her exam questions.

Their work aligned with their deep encodings. You can think of encodings as built-in tendencies or aptitudes dormant in each of us. All of us have them, yet no two people have the same combination. Encodings can’t be willed into existence – they can only be uncovered through lived experience. Eventually, you will find the one thing that makes them shine. This is your personal hedgehog: a singular pursuit that absorbs your energy for years or even decades.

For Toni Morrison, it was writing. For Barbara Tuchmann, it was history. For Barbara McClintock, it was genetics. Discovering your personal hedgehog will involve trial and error, missteps, and happy accidents. You might get lucky and stumble upon your hedgehog early in life. Or you might be searching for a few decades.

The author found that about a third of remarkable people didn’t find their first hedgehog until their 30s or 40s – some even later. Many people also have several hedgehogs throughout their lives, graduating from one big project to the next as new encodings reveal themselves. Take NFL Hall of Famer Alan Page, who later served as a Minnesota Supreme Court justice, then launched an education foundation for students of color. Each of these transitions were seeded long before they happened, and each of them aligned with the encodings present for him at that stage of life. While you never lose any of your encodings, you may imagine them as a constellation of stars. Your life is the movable frame that determines which ones are visible at any given moment.

Things flow when your pursuits align with the encodings currently “in frame”. The important thing is learning to recognize when that happens – and equally, when you’ve drifted away from your encodings. Encodings are also the reason why it’s so dangerous to copy other people’s blueprint for success. What fuels someone else’s inner fire will not necessarily work for you.

Toni Morrison discovered she did her best writing in small bursts, in between child care and her day job. By contrast, Barbara Tuchmann needed long uninterrupted blocks of silence to do her work, using index cards to keep her thoughts in order. On the surface, they were doing the same type of work – expressing their ideas in writing – but they used completely different modes to keep their fire alive. Following your deep encodings creates an inner flywheel.

#### Chapter 3 of 6

## The cliffs of life are inevitable

You find something you love, you do a lot of it, you get better at it, you experience the satisfaction of mastery, and then you want to do even more of it. Suddenly, there’s no need for external rewards, recognition or discipline. The flywheel runs all by itself, fueled by an ever-burning love of doing. That doesn’t mean you need to be so obsessed with your pursuit that it’s the only thing you care about.

In his research, the author found that 53 percent of remarkable people placed high priority on their marriage or parental roles. Moreover, 88 percent of people had side passions. Take Robert Plant, the Led Zeppelin frontman, who was also a passionate vice president of his hometown football club. Unfortunately, following your encodings also doesn’t guarantee your life free of strife and struggle. Nobody escapes the unavoidable friction, frustration, and tedium that exists even inside the work you love most. Despite hundreds of shows and a deep love for his work, Led Zeppelin Jimmy Page got wound into a knot of stress before every performance.

Every once in a while, you’ll find yourself facing a cliff. A cliff is a significant life event – the loss of a job, a death in the family, a prolonged illness – that disrupts your current path and forces you to rethink what’s next. Not a single person the author studied had a “cliff-free” life. The interesting part is the different ways that people respond to cliffs. Take the examples of Cardiss Collins and Maryon Pittman Allen. Both lost their husbands early.

Both were then pressured to take up their husband’s congressional seats. Collins, who was initially reluctant to take the job, discovered her natural aptitude for legislative work. She ended up serving 12 terms. Pittman Allen continued to feel fundamentally out of place in politics. She only served six months, and later found her passion in restoring antique bridal gowns. Same cliff, different results.

For Collins, the cliff revealed a hidden encoding that primed her to become an exceptional politician. For Pittman Allen, the cliff was a reminder that what she really cared about lay beyond politics. Cliffs are a near-universal feature of the human experience. They don’t hinder your journey. Quite the opposite: they can reveal hidden encodings and spark remarkable transformations. But only if you navigate them right.

#### Chapter 4 of 6

## Take small steps to clear the fog

After surviving a cliff, many people find themselves enveloped by a thick fog – a period of disorientation where progress is utterly uncertain. Actor Michael J. Fox kept his Parkinson’s diagnosis private for seven years. He wrestled with what it meant for his career, family and identity.

His path out of this fog was not linear. He quit drinking, stepped away from movies to do more TV shows, and only gradually found his new role as a Parkinson’s advocate. His example shows that while cliffs are sudden, clarity is gradual. From the outside, life changes look sudden and binary. But from the inside, rebuilding yourself after a crisis is a slow, overlapping, and iterative process. And that’s a good thing.

Consider American publisher Katharine Graham. She didn’t necessarily leap into her new leadership role of The Washington Post Company after her husband’s suicide. Rather, she took things one step at a time, hiring the right people and building financial literacy before making any big decisions. By the time she made the influential choice to publish the Watergate reports, she’d already had seven years of honing this craft. This is the idea of “simplex stepping”, inspired by mathematician George Dantzig’s simplex algorithm. When you can’t see the full terrain, take the best next step available, then reassess.

You don’t always need a plan, goal or destination. You just need to keep moving, using your existing encodings as your compass – and preparing to discover new ones. When Meryl Streep feared her career was fading at 40, she deliberately pushed herself into new roles – stepping into comedy, action and musicals. At first, critics resisted this apparent turn away from her established identity as a dramatic actress. But Streep ignored these outside expectations, only to occasionally prove her competence by returning to more nuanced, emotionally complex characters. Today, in her mid-seventies, she remains one of the most iconic figures on the big screen.

Yet evolving as a person isn’t always visible as such clear outward success. Many post-fog transformations are largely invisible to the public eye. Actress Debra Winger, who had similar early-career success, made a conscious decision to step away from Hollywood. She focused on raising a child, caring for her dying mother, and exploring spirituality.

She kept honoring her acting talent by appearing in smaller stage productions. This new, quieter life isn’t a downgrade to her. It’s a conscious choice keeping her inner fire alive.

#### Chapter 5 of 6

## Your fire doesn’t have to fade with age

Cliffs and fogs can completely transform us as people, at any stage of life. That could be why many people in the study have another interesting thing in common: they did their best work late. Toni Morrison published one of the books central to her Nobel Prize at 61. Robert Plant earned 86 percent of his Grammy nominations, and all eight wins, after turning 50.

Katharine Graham wrote one of the finest CEO memoirs ever written – in her 70s. Or take the famous example of Founding Father Benjamin Franklin, whose ambitions were repeatedly frustrated by the political climate of the time. He was already 70 when he joined the Declaration committee. Properly tended, your inner fire can burn for decades. The self-knowledge that comes with age allows you to tend your fire in just the right way. First, you will have discovered more of your raw encodings.

Second, you’ll have the cumulative experience of recognizing your patterns, learning from your mistakes, and refining your skills. And third, you’ll have trust, respect and community built over a lifetime to act as a multiplier of your actions. The quality of your inner fire may also change over the years, transforming from urgent, almost painful ambition toward something warmer, more joyful, and freely chosen. Early interests that went unattended for decades can become the fuel for later reinvention. Consider the life story of lawyer Charles Colson, Nixon’s feared political enforcer who was one of the people indicted during the Watergate scandal. After prison, he turned his back on cut-throat politics and instead built Prison Fellowship, a nonprofit dedicated to helping inmates and advocating for prison reform.

He never tried to erase or rewrite his Watergate past – he simply kept moving forward. Nearly every subject studied by the author embodied this quality: continually moving forward, right until their bodies gave out. Voluntarily taking on new and bigger responsibilities – even when they felt scary – proved a reliable mechanism for achieving this. After his time in prison, Colson chose to take on new responsibilities that better aligned with his values. Having learned from previous mistakes, he completely reinvented himself, incidentally changing the tone of his entire life story. So next time you are confronted with the belief that creativity peaks in youth, consider the advantages of age.

#### Chapter 6 of 6

## Living with meaning

Let’s return to the question from the beginning, slightly rephrased: How do we live a meaningful life? The answer is quite simple: You uncover your deep encodings and follow them, relentlessly. This is what all the people in the author’s study had in common. They didn’t plan to have an impact.

Rather, meaning grew organically from following their passion, taking small steps, and evolving through unforeseen change. Similarly, their happiness seemed to be a byproduct of the way they lived, not something they chased directly. But it’s important to note that they all discovered their encodings in different ways, along different time frames, and with different outcomes – some visible as outward success, others hidden from the public as quiet growth. Sometimes, their lives were characterized by excelling at one big thing. Sometimes, it was a confluence or sequence of multiple endeavors in radically different arenas that led them to greatness. Often, important personal hedgehogs didn’t reveal themselves until late in life.

The lesson we can take from studying these people is that there really is no blueprint. There’s no step-by-step recipe or one-size-fits-all instruction to create a meaningful life. But we can deduce this: Living a meaningful life isn’t about money, fame or recognition. It’s about discovering your deep encodings, trusting them, and committing fully. What are your encodings telling you? Rather than chasing definitive answers, you can start by asking more questions.

What does happiness mean to you? What does success feel like to you? When do you feel most alive? What activities drain you?

When does life “click into frame”? When are you drifting away from your purpose? Become a scientist of yourself and try to explore these questions non-judgmentally, clinically, and curiously. What emerges from your exploration may surprise you.

#### Conclusion

## Final summary

The main takeaway of this Blink to What to Make of a Life by Jim Collins is that a meaningful life emerges from honoring your deep encodings. History’s most remarkable people didn’t follow a masterplan. But they have a few things in common. First, they discovered their deep encodings – innate aptitudes that made certain work feel less like effort and more like oxygen.

Second, they found big projects that brought those encodings to the forefront. These personal hedgehogs ignited their inner fire like nothing else. Third, they evolved with every cliff and fog they faced – moving forward with small steps even when the direction was unclear at times. The lesson we can learn from figures like Toni Morrison, Meryl Streep and Charles Colson is that we should stop asking what a meaningful life looks like, and start asking what makes us feel alive.

The next step is to follow that – wherever it leads. Thanks so much for listening. See you in the next Blink.
